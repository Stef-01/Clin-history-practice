Clinical medicine / bedside / rapid reference

Clinical Presentation
Approaches

A compact bedside handbook for assessment, targeted investigation, imaging, management and prioritised differentials.

One presentation at a time. Every approach follows the same vertical sequence so you can move from immediate threat recognition to a focused working plan without scanning across a wide table.

01

Stabilise

Identify airway, breathing, circulation, neurological or exposure threats before completing the diagnostic work-up.

02

Localise

Use focused history, examination, bedside tests and imaging to define the likely source and severity.

03

Act

Start time-critical management while investigations continue, then reassess the trajectory.

How to use this handbook

Move down the case, not across the page.

Each presentation is a single vertical sequence: bedside examination, investigations, imaging or procedures, management, then differentials. Within each system, the order moves broadly from immediately dangerous presentations to urgent and lower-acuity problems.

UNSTABLE PATIENT: A-E assessment, monitoring, IV access, bedside glucose, early escalation and simultaneous treatment.
01 / STABILISE

Treat physiological threats first

Do not delay airway support, oxygen for hypoxaemia, haemorrhage control, decompression, antibiotics or other time-critical care to complete a perfect history.

02 / FOCUS

Define the clinical pattern

Clarify onset, severity, triggers, associated symptoms, risk factors and treatment already given. Examine the affected and adjacent systems.

03 / TEST

Order investigations with a question

Use bedside tests and bloods to identify physiological threat, organ dysfunction and likely cause. Avoid indiscriminate panels.

04 / IMAGE

Image with purpose

State whether imaging must exclude an emergency, define anatomy, locate a source, stage disease or guide a procedure.

05 / MANAGE

Start care while investigating

Analgesia, resuscitation, isolation, antimicrobial therapy, anticoagulation, decompression or source control may be time-critical.

06 / REASSESS

Let trajectory override labels

Repeat observations and examination. A changing patient overrides a reassuring early test or the initial diagnostic label.

System index

Ordered by clinical urgency.

02

Resuscitation, bleeding, obstruction and perforation first, followed by hepatobiliary and bowel presentations.

03

Obstruction, acute renal deterioration and time-critical genital presentations first, then chronic urinary and renal problems.

04

Endocrine masses with acute neurological, airway or hormonal risk first, followed by systemic and hormonal presentations.

05

Septic joints, unstable fractures and neurological compromise first, followed by inflammatory and soft-tissue disorders.

06

Seizure, stroke, raised intracranial pressure and structural lesions first, then balance, falls and sensory symptoms.

07

Major burns, contaminated wounds and acute tissue failure first, followed by lesions and pruritus.

08

09

Sepsis and high-risk fever syndromes first, followed by recurrent and unexplained infection patterns.

Bleeding, marrow failure and dangerous cell-count abnormalities first, then lymphoid and splenic presentations.

Oncological compression and metabolic emergencies first, followed by communication at critical points in care.

01.01

Stridor

Time-critical first
Clinical priorityCall anaesthetics and ENT immediately, keep the patient calm and prepare a difficult-airway plan. Do not force examination or send an unstable patient to imaging.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Emergency: keep patient calm, call anaesthetics/ENT, assess airway without provoking complete obstruction.
  • Determine inspiratory, expiratory or biphasic stridor; onset, voice change, drooling, dysphagia, allergy, foreign body, recent intubation and neck surgery.
  • Observe work of breathing, oxygenation, tracheal position, neck swelling and ability to handle secretions.
02 / Investigations
  • Minimal tests before airway is secured if severe.
  • VBG/ABG, FBC, CRP and cultures after stabilisation when infection suspected.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • Flexible nasendoscopy by experienced clinician if safe.
  • Neck/chest X-ray or CT only in stable patients.
  • Direct laryngoscopy or bronchoscopy may be diagnostic and therapeutic.
04 / Management
  • Call anaesthetics and ENT immediately, keep the patient calm and prepare a difficult-airway plan. Do not force examination or send an unstable patient to imaging.
  • Give high-flow oxygen and cause-directed emergency treatment, including intramuscular adrenaline for anaphylaxis and nebulised adrenaline plus corticosteroid for selected oedematous airway conditions.
  • Definitive management may require awake intubation, rigid bronchoscopy, surgical airway or urgent drainage of a deep neck process.
05 / Differentials
  • Must not miss: anaphylaxis, foreign body, epiglottitis, deep neck infection, bilateral vocal cord paralysis, expanding neck haematoma.
  • Other: croup, post-intubation oedema/stenosis, laryngeal tumour, tracheomalacia.
01.02

Dyspnoea

Time-critical first
Clinical priorityPosition upright, give oxygen only when hypoxaemic, establish monitoring and treat immediately reversible threats such as tension pneumothorax, anaphylaxis, pulmonary oedema or severe bronchospasm.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Immediate: A-E, SpO2, respiratory rate, work of breathing, ability to speak, mental state and haemodynamic stability.
  • History: sudden versus progressive, exertional versus rest, orthopnoea, PND, cough, fever, wheeze, pleuritic pain, haemoptysis, exposures and medication history.
  • Examine airway, trachea, chest expansion, percussion, breath sounds, JVP, heart, oedema, calves and signs of anaemia or neuromuscular weakness.
02 / Investigations
  • ECG, bedside glucose, VBG/ABG if severe, FBC, UEC, CRP, troponin and BNP when indicated.
  • Viral testing, cultures and lactate when infection or sepsis suspected.
  • Peak flow or spirometry only when safe and appropriate.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • CXR and bedside lung or cardiac ultrasound.
  • CTPA for suspected PE; CT chest for interstitial, malignant or occult pulmonary disease.
  • Echocardiography for suspected cardiac failure, valvular disease or pulmonary hypertension.
04 / Management
  • Position upright, give oxygen only when hypoxaemic, establish monitoring and treat immediately reversible threats such as tension pneumothorax, anaphylaxis, pulmonary oedema or severe bronchospasm.
  • Use cause-specific treatment: bronchodilators and corticosteroids for obstructive disease, antibiotics for infection, diuresis and vasodilators for pulmonary oedema, anticoagulation for PE when appropriate.
  • Escalate early for high-flow oxygen, non-invasive ventilation or intubation when work of breathing, gas exchange or mental state worsens.
05 / Differentials
  • Must not miss: upper airway obstruction, tension pneumothorax, PE, acute pulmonary oedema, severe asthma, anaphylaxis, ACS, metabolic acidosis.
  • Common: asthma or COPD, pneumonia, heart failure, pleural effusion, anaemia, deconditioning.
  • Other: ILD, pulmonary hypertension, neuromuscular disease, obesity hypoventilation, anxiety.
01.03

Chest pain

Time-critical first
Clinical priorityTreat as time-critical until ACS, aortic catastrophe, PE and pneumothorax are excluded. Use monitored care, IV access and early senior review.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Immediate: A-E, full observations, cardiac monitor, IV access; treat instability while assessing.
  • Characterise onset, site, radiation, quality, duration, triggers, exertional pattern and associated dyspnoea, diaphoresis, nausea, syncope or haemoptysis.
  • Examine pulses and BP in both arms if dissection suspected; JVP, heart sounds, murmurs, chest, chest wall, abdomen and calves.
02 / Investigations
  • 12-lead ECG within 10 minutes and repeat if symptoms evolve.
  • Serial high-sensitivity troponin using local pathway.
  • FBC, UEC, glucose, LFT, lipase; coagulation if anticoagulated; VBG/ABG and lactate if unwell.
  • D-dimer only when pre-test probability supports its use; pregnancy test where relevant.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • CXR for pulmonary, aortic or alternative pathology.
  • CT pulmonary angiography for suspected PE; CT aortogram for suspected dissection.
  • Echocardiography for shock, new murmur, pericardial disease or heart failure.
  • Coronary angiography or CT coronary angiography according to ACS pathway and risk.
04 / Management
  • Treat as time-critical until ACS, aortic catastrophe, PE and pneumothorax are excluded. Use monitored care, IV access and early senior review.
  • Give syndrome-directed therapy under the local pathway, including antiplatelet and reperfusion decisions for ACS, anticoagulation for confirmed PE, or immediate surgical referral for dissection.
  • Provide analgesia, correct hypoxaemia, manage haemodynamic instability and arrange disposition based on serial ECG, troponin and risk assessment.
05 / Differentials
  • Must not miss: ACS, aortic dissection, PE, tension pneumothorax, cardiac tamponade, oesophageal rupture.
  • Common: reflux or oesophagitis, musculoskeletal pain, pneumonia, pleurisy, anxiety.
  • Other: pericarditis, myocarditis, biliary disease, pancreatitis, herpes zoster.
01.04

Syncope

Time-critical first
Clinical priorityPlace supine, assess A-E, glucose, ECG and injury. Admit or monitor patients with exertional syncope, abnormal ECG, structural heart disease, haemorrhage, persistent hypotension or neurological deficit.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Confirm transient loss of consciousness with rapid onset, short duration and spontaneous recovery.
  • Witness history: posture, prodrome, pallor, movements, duration, injury, recovery and confusion.
  • Orthostatic BP, cardiovascular and neurological examination; look for bleeding and pregnancy-related causes.
02 / Investigations
  • 12-lead ECG and bedside glucose for all.
  • FBC, UEC, troponin, pregnancy test and other tests guided by history.
  • Avoid routine broad blood panels without indication.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • Telemetry or ambulatory monitor if arrhythmia suspected.
  • Echocardiography for murmur, abnormal ECG or structural disease.
  • CT brain only for focal deficit, significant head injury or alternative neurological concern.
  • Tilt testing or implantable loop recorder in selected recurrent cases.
04 / Management
  • Place supine, assess A-E, glucose, ECG and injury. Admit or monitor patients with exertional syncope, abnormal ECG, structural heart disease, haemorrhage, persistent hypotension or neurological deficit.
  • Treat the cause, including fluids for volume loss, rhythm management, management of bleeding, PE or ACS, and medication adjustment for orthostatic hypotension.
  • Low-risk reflex syncope is managed with education, hydration, trigger avoidance and safety advice.
05 / Differentials
  • Must not miss: ventricular arrhythmia, high-grade block, aortic stenosis, PE, haemorrhage, ectopic pregnancy, subarachnoid haemorrhage.
  • Common: vasovagal, orthostatic hypotension, medication-related.
  • Mimics: seizure, hypoglycaemia, psychogenic pseudosyncope, falls without loss of consciousness.
01.05

Haemoptysis

Time-critical first
Clinical priorityMassive or physiologically significant bleeding requires airway protection, resuscitation, reversal of coagulopathy and urgent respiratory, interventional radiology and thoracic input.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Confirm lower respiratory source and distinguish from haematemesis or nasopharyngeal bleeding.
  • Estimate volume, rate and airway risk; A-E, oxygenation, haemodynamics and two large-bore IVs if significant.
  • Assess infection, malignancy, anticoagulation, VTE and TB risk; examine chest and ENT tract.
02 / Investigations
  • FBC, coagulation, UEC, LFT, CRP, group and hold/crossmatch if major.
  • Sputum culture, TB testing and cytology when indicated.
  • ABG and lactate if unstable.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • CXR initially.
  • CT chest with angiographic protocol for significant or unexplained haemoptysis.
  • Bronchoscopy for airway control, localisation, ongoing bleeding or endobronchial lesion.
  • Interventional radiology for bronchial artery embolisation.
04 / Management
  • Massive or physiologically significant bleeding requires airway protection, resuscitation, reversal of coagulopathy and urgent respiratory, interventional radiology and thoracic input.
  • Position the bleeding lung down when the side is known. Consider early bronchoscopy and bronchial artery embolisation.
  • Treat the source, including infection, PE, malignancy or inflammatory disease; do not discharge unexplained recurrent haemoptysis without follow-up.
05 / Differentials
  • Must not miss: massive haemoptysis, lung cancer, PE, TB, diffuse alveolar haemorrhage.
  • Common: bronchitis, pneumonia, bronchiectasis.
  • Other: mitral stenosis, pulmonary oedema, anticoagulant-related bleeding, AV malformation.
01.06

Pleuritic chest pain

Time-critical first
Clinical priorityExclude PE, pneumothorax, pneumonia, pericarditis and ACS before labelling pain as benign pleurisy.
01 / Bedside examination
  • A-E and assess for hypoxaemia or shock.
  • Pain worsened by inspiration or cough; ask about dyspnoea, haemoptysis, fever, VTE risk, trauma and autoimmune disease.
  • Examine lungs, pleural rub, chest wall, heart, calves and abdomen.
02 / Investigations
  • ECG and troponin to exclude cardiac causes when appropriate.
  • FBC, CRP, D-dimer within a validated PE pathway; ABG/VBG if severe.
  • Autoimmune tests only if supported by clinical pattern.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • CXR and bedside ultrasound.
  • CTPA for PE; CT chest for occult pleural or pulmonary disease.
  • Echocardiography for pericardial effusion or right-heart strain.
04 / Management
  • Exclude PE, pneumothorax, pneumonia, pericarditis and ACS before labelling pain as benign pleurisy.
  • Provide analgesia and treat the cause, including anticoagulation for PE, drainage for significant pneumothorax, or antimicrobials for pneumonia.
  • Escalate for hypoxaemia, haemodynamic instability, significant effusion or persistent diagnostic uncertainty.
05 / Differentials
  • Must not miss: PE, pneumothorax, pneumonia with sepsis, pericarditis, ACS, aortic dissection.
  • Common: viral pleurisy, chest wall pain.
  • Other: pleural effusion, malignancy, autoimmune serositis, subphrenic pathology.
01.07

Painful limb

Time-critical first
Clinical priorityCheck the six Ps and compartment signs immediately. Acute limb ischaemia, compartment syndrome and necrotising infection require urgent surgical or vascular review.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Immediate neurovascular examination: colour, temperature, capillary refill, pulses, motor and sensory function.
  • Assess pain severity, mechanism, swelling, erythema, wounds, passive stretch pain and systemic toxicity.
  • Examine joints above and below; compare contralateral limb.
02 / Investigations
  • FBC, CRP, UEC, CK, lactate and coagulation according to suspected cause.
  • Blood cultures if septic; D-dimer only within a validated DVT pathway.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • X-ray for trauma or bony pathology.
  • Doppler ultrasound for DVT or arterial flow.
  • CT angiography for acute limb ischaemia; MRI for osteomyelitis, deep infection or occult pathology.
  • Compartment pressure measurement if diagnosis uncertain and suspicion remains high.
04 / Management
  • Check the six Ps and compartment signs immediately. Acute limb ischaemia, compartment syndrome and necrotising infection require urgent surgical or vascular review.
  • Provide analgesia, keep the patient nil by mouth, obtain IV access and avoid compression or elevation when arterial occlusion is suspected.
  • Use cause-specific treatment, including anticoagulation for acute arterial thrombosis when directed, fasciotomy for compartment syndrome, antibiotics and debridement for infection, or immobilisation for injury.
05 / Differentials
  • Must not miss: acute limb ischaemia, compartment syndrome, necrotising fasciitis, septic arthritis, DVT.
  • Common: fracture, sprain, cellulitis, gout, muscle strain, venous insufficiency.
  • Other: radiculopathy, peripheral neuropathy, complex regional pain syndrome, malignancy.
01.08

Palpitations

Focused clinical approach
Clinical priorityUnstable tachyarrhythmia requires immediate resuscitation and synchronised cardioversion under the local ALS pathway.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Clarify sudden versus gradual onset, regularity, duration, triggers, exertion and associated syncope, chest pain or dyspnoea.
  • Pulse rate and rhythm, orthostatic vitals, cardiac examination, signs of thyrotoxicosis, anaemia and stimulant use.
  • Capture rhythm during symptoms if possible.
02 / Investigations
  • 12-lead ECG; electrolytes, magnesium, FBC, TSH and pregnancy test where relevant.
  • Troponin if ischaemic symptoms; drug screen only when it changes management.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • Telemetry or ambulatory monitor selected by symptom frequency.
  • Echocardiography for structural disease, abnormal ECG or exertional symptoms.
  • Exercise testing when exertional arrhythmia or ischaemia suspected.
04 / Management
  • Unstable tachyarrhythmia requires immediate resuscitation and synchronised cardioversion under the local ALS pathway.
  • For stable patients, identify rhythm, correct potassium, magnesium, hypoxia and triggers, then use rhythm-specific rate or rhythm control.
  • Assess stroke risk and anticoagulation for atrial fibrillation, and arrange ambulatory monitoring or electrophysiology review for recurrent unexplained episodes.
05 / Differentials
  • Arrhythmic: AF/flutter, SVT, ectopy, VT, bradyarrhythmia, long QT.
  • Physiological: exercise, fever, pregnancy.
  • Systemic: anaemia, thyrotoxicosis, hypoglycaemia, sepsis.
  • Substances: caffeine, alcohol, stimulants, medications.
  • Anxiety or panic after organic causes considered.
01.09

Wheeze

Time-critical first
Clinical priorityAssess severity and silent chest, exhaustion, hypoxaemia or altered consciousness. Give inhaled bronchodilator therapy promptly and systemic corticosteroid for significant asthma or COPD exacerbation.
01 / Bedside examination
  • A-E, ability to speak, respiratory fatigue, silent chest, SpO2 and peak flow if safe.
  • Assess episodic pattern, triggers, atopy, smoking, occupational exposure, medications and cardiac symptoms.
  • Chest examination plus upper airway and cardiac assessment.
02 / Investigations
  • Peak expiratory flow; VBG/ABG if severe or tiring.
  • FBC/eosinophils, viral testing and CRP if indicated.
  • Spirometry with bronchodilator response when stable.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • CXR if first episode, focal signs, fever, trauma or poor response.
  • CT chest for focal/persistent wheeze or suspected central obstruction.
  • Echocardiography when cardiac wheeze suspected.
04 / Management
  • Assess severity and silent chest, exhaustion, hypoxaemia or altered consciousness. Give inhaled bronchodilator therapy promptly and systemic corticosteroid for significant asthma or COPD exacerbation.
  • Treat anaphylaxis with intramuscular adrenaline. Treat pulmonary oedema or foreign body rather than assuming all wheeze is asthma.
  • Escalate for continuous therapy, magnesium, ventilatory support or ICU review when response is poor.
05 / Differentials
  • Asthma, COPD, viral bronchitis.
  • Must not miss: anaphylaxis, foreign body, severe asthma with impending arrest, pulmonary oedema.
  • Other: bronchiectasis, central airway tumour, vocal cord dysfunction, bronchiolitis.
01.10

Limb swelling

Focused clinical approach
Clinical priorityEscalate unilateral acute swelling with pain, cyanosis, dyspnoea or pulse deficit for urgent DVT, PE or vascular assessment.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Compare both limbs: distribution, pitting, erythema, warmth, tenderness, skin changes, wounds and pulses.
  • Assess onset, immobility, surgery, cancer, pregnancy, medications, heart, renal and liver symptoms.
  • Look for respiratory distress, JVP elevation, ascites and lymphadenopathy.
02 / Investigations
  • FBC, UEC, LFT, albumin, CRP; urinalysis and urine ACR if systemic oedema.
  • D-dimer only when DVT probability is low or intermediate.
  • BNP, thyroid tests or coagulation studies when clinically indicated.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • Compression ultrasound for suspected DVT.
  • Echocardiography and CXR for heart failure.
  • Abdominal or pelvic imaging if proximal venous obstruction or mass suspected.
  • Lymphoscintigraphy rarely, after specialist review.
04 / Management
  • Escalate unilateral acute swelling with pain, cyanosis, dyspnoea or pulse deficit for urgent DVT, PE or vascular assessment.
  • Treat the cause: anticoagulation for confirmed DVT, diuresis and heart failure therapy for systemic fluid overload, antibiotics for cellulitis, and compression for chronic venous or lymphatic disease once arterial disease is excluded.
  • Elevate the limb, protect skin and address provoking factors, medications and mobility.
05 / Differentials
  • Unilateral: DVT, cellulitis, ruptured Baker cyst, trauma, venous insufficiency, lymphoedema, pelvic obstruction.
  • Bilateral: heart failure, renal or liver disease, medications, hypoalbuminaemia, venous insufficiency.
  • Must not miss: phlegmasia, compartment syndrome, necrotising infection.
01.11

Heart murmur

Focused clinical approach
Clinical priorityIf new with shock, pulmonary oedema, fever or chest pain, obtain urgent echocardiography and cardiology review for acute valve failure or infective endocarditis.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Determine timing, site, radiation, intensity, pitch and response to position or manoeuvres.
  • Assess pulse character, BP, JVP, apex, heart sounds, signs of heart failure, endocarditis and peripheral emboli.
  • Ask about exertional dyspnoea, chest pain, syncope, fever, IVDU, prosthetic valves and rheumatic history.
02 / Investigations
  • ECG and FBC; CRP/ESR and blood cultures if endocarditis suspected.
  • BNP if heart failure; haemolysis tests if prosthetic valve dysfunction suspected.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • Transthoracic echocardiography for a pathological, new or symptomatic murmur.
  • Transoesophageal echo for prosthetic valves, suspected endocarditis or inadequate TTE.
  • CXR if heart failure or structural enlargement suspected.
04 / Management
  • If new with shock, pulmonary oedema, fever or chest pain, obtain urgent echocardiography and cardiology review for acute valve failure or infective endocarditis.
  • Treat congestion and haemodynamic compromise while defining lesion severity. Avoid major preload or afterload changes without understanding the valve lesion.
  • Refer significant, symptomatic or uncertain murmurs for formal echocardiography and specialist follow-up; manage cardiovascular risk and endocarditis source where present.
05 / Differentials
  • Systolic: aortic stenosis, mitral regurgitation, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, VSD, flow murmur.
  • Diastolic: aortic regurgitation, mitral stenosis.
  • Continuous: PDA, AV fistula.
  • Mimics: venous hum, transmitted bruit.
01.12

Cough

Focused clinical approach
Clinical priorityTreat immediate compromise, hypoxaemia, sepsis or significant haemoptysis first.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Classify acute, subacute or chronic; dry versus productive; assess fever, dyspnoea, wheeze, reflux, rhinitis, weight loss and haemoptysis.
  • Medication and exposure review, especially ACE inhibitor, smoking, occupation and aspiration risk.
  • Examine upper airway, chest, heart and signs of clubbing or systemic disease.
02 / Investigations
  • Pulse oximetry; viral testing, FBC and CRP if systemically unwell.
  • Sputum microscopy and culture for significant productive cough, immunocompromise or treatment failure.
  • Spirometry with bronchodilator testing for chronic cough when safe.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • CXR for chronic cough, red flags or suspected pneumonia.
  • CT chest for abnormal CXR or persistent unexplained cough.
  • ENT assessment, nasendoscopy, bronchoscopy or reflux testing in selected cases.
04 / Management
  • Treat immediate compromise, hypoxaemia, sepsis or significant haemoptysis first.
  • Manage the identified cause: antibiotics only for likely bacterial infection, inhaled therapy for asthma or COPD, reflux or upper-airway treatment when supported, and cessation of ACE inhibitor or smoking where relevant.
  • Persistent cough, red flags or abnormal imaging require respiratory review and further investigation.
05 / Differentials
  • Acute: viral URTI, pneumonia, asthma, aspiration, pulmonary oedema, PE.
  • Chronic: upper airway cough syndrome, asthma/eosinophilic bronchitis, reflux, smoking/COPD, ACE inhibitor.
  • Must not miss: lung cancer, TB, ILD, bronchiectasis.
01.13

Lung nodule on chest X-ray

Focused clinical approach
Clinical priorityCompare prior imaging and obtain diagnostic thin-slice CT rather than relying on the radiograph alone.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Review symptoms and cancer risk: age, smoking, occupational exposure, prior malignancy, haemoptysis and weight loss.
  • Compare with prior imaging and assess for infection or inflammatory disease.
  • Full respiratory examination and lymph node assessment.
02 / Investigations
  • Usually no broad blood panel is diagnostic.
  • FBC/CRP if infection suspected; TB or fungal testing according to epidemiology.
  • Pre-procedure coagulation and renal function when biopsy or contrast planned.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • Thin-slice CT chest is the next test to confirm size, morphology, calcification and growth.
  • Risk-stratified CT surveillance, PET-CT or tissue sampling.
  • Biopsy method depends on location: bronchoscopy, EBUS or CT-guided biopsy.
04 / Management
  • Compare prior imaging and obtain diagnostic thin-slice CT rather than relying on the radiograph alone.
  • Stratify malignancy risk using size, morphology, growth, smoking history and patient factors, then follow a validated surveillance pathway.
  • Refer suspicious, enlarging or high-risk nodules to a lung nodule or thoracic multidisciplinary service for PET, biopsy or resection planning.
05 / Differentials
  • Malignant: primary lung cancer, metastasis, carcinoid.
  • Benign: granuloma, hamartoma, scar, intrapulmonary lymph node.
  • Infective/inflammatory: TB, fungal disease, rheumatoid nodule, granulomatosis with polyangiitis.
  • Pseudonodule: nipple shadow, vessel or skin lesion.
01.14

Excessive daytime sleepiness

Focused clinical approach
Clinical priorityAddress immediate safety, including driving, operating machinery and sedating medications.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Quantify with Epworth Sleepiness Scale; distinguish sleepiness from fatigue.
  • Assess sleep duration, shift work, snoring, witnessed apnoea, cataplexy, restless legs, mood, medications and substance use.
  • BMI, neck circumference, upper airway, BP, cardiorespiratory and neurological examination.
02 / Investigations
  • Sleep diary and medication review.
  • FBC, UEC, LFT, TSH, iron studies, B12 and glucose when secondary causes suspected.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • Home sleep apnoea test or polysomnography.
  • Multiple sleep latency test after adequate sleep and exclusion of sleep-disordered breathing when narcolepsy suspected.
  • Imaging only for focal neurological or hypothalamic concern.
04 / Management
  • Address immediate safety, including driving, operating machinery and sedating medications.
  • Treat the cause: optimise sleep opportunity and hygiene, manage obstructive sleep apnoea with weight reduction and positive airway pressure when indicated, and treat mood, endocrine or neurological contributors.
  • Refer suspected narcolepsy, central hypersomnolence or complex sleep-disordered breathing to sleep medicine.
05 / Differentials
  • Insufficient sleep, obstructive sleep apnoea, sedating drugs, depression.
  • Narcolepsy, idiopathic hypersomnia, circadian rhythm disorder, restless legs/periodic limb movement.
  • Systemic disease: hypothyroidism, anaemia, renal or liver failure.
01.15

Snoring

Focused clinical approach
Clinical priorityScreen for obstructive sleep apnoea, daytime impairment and driving risk rather than treating snoring as cosmetic.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Assess witnessed apnoea, choking, unrefreshing sleep, daytime sleepiness, resistant hypertension and driving risk.
  • STOP-BANG screening; alcohol, sedatives and nasal obstruction.
  • BMI, neck circumference, craniofacial anatomy, tonsils, nasal airway, BP and cardiopulmonary examination.
02 / Investigations
  • No routine blood tests for simple snoring.
  • FBC, TSH, bicarbonate or ABG if hypoventilation or secondary causes suspected.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • Home sleep study or polysomnography if obstructive sleep apnoea suspected.
  • ENT nasendoscopy for structural obstruction.
  • Imaging rarely required.
04 / Management
  • Screen for obstructive sleep apnoea, daytime impairment and driving risk rather than treating snoring as cosmetic.
  • Use weight management, positional measures, alcohol and sedative reduction, and nasal obstruction treatment where relevant.
  • Offer positive airway pressure, mandibular advancement or selected ENT surgery according to sleep study findings and specialist assessment.
05 / Differentials
  • Primary snoring, obstructive sleep apnoea, upper airway resistance syndrome.
  • Nasal obstruction, tonsillar hypertrophy, obesity hypoventilation, sedative/alcohol effect.
  • Central sleep apnoea when neurological, cardiac or opioid risk exists.
02.01

Perforated viscus

Time-critical first
Clinical priorityImmediate A-E resuscitation, large-bore IV access, nil by mouth, broad-spectrum IV antibiotics, analgesia and urgent surgical review.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Surgical emergency: A-E, NBM, IV access, analgesia, antibiotics and urgent surgical review.
  • Sudden severe pain, rigid abdomen, guarding, rebound, absent bowel sounds and sepsis.
  • Consider recent endoscopy, ulcer disease, diverticulitis, malignancy, steroid or NSAID use.
02 / Investigations
  • FBC, UEC, LFT, CRP, lipase, coagulation, group and hold/crossmatch, lactate and blood cultures if septic.
  • Pregnancy test where relevant.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • Erect CXR may show free subdiaphragmatic gas but a normal film does not exclude perforation.
  • Urgent contrast CT abdomen/pelvis is preferred when stable enough.
  • Do not delay surgery for imaging in profound instability with convincing signs.
04 / Management
  • Immediate A-E resuscitation, large-bore IV access, nil by mouth, broad-spectrum IV antibiotics, analgesia and urgent surgical review.
  • Use nasogastric decompression and urinary catheterisation where appropriate. Correct shock and electrolyte disturbance without delaying source control.
  • Definitive treatment is urgent operative or selected interventional source control.
05 / Differentials
  • Perforated peptic ulcer, diverticulum, appendicitis, malignancy, ischaemic bowel, trauma or iatrogenic perforation.
  • Gynaecological or urinary rupture.
  • Mimics: pancreatitis, mesenteric ischaemia, severe gastroenteritis, metabolic crisis.
02.02

Haematemesis and melaena

Time-critical first
Clinical priorityResuscitate with large-bore IV access, blood products according to haemorrhage protocol and restrictive transfusion strategy unless specific factors dictate otherwise.
01 / Bedside examination
  • A-E, shock index, mental state and ongoing bleeding; two large-bore IVs, group and crossmatch.
  • History of liver disease, NSAIDs, anticoagulants, alcohol, prior ulcer or varices.
  • Abdominal exam, stigmata of chronic liver disease and rectal examination.
02 / Investigations
  • FBC, UEC, LFT, INR/APTT, fibrinogen, group and crossmatch, VBG/ABG and lactate.
  • Serial haemoglobin is more useful than a single early value.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • Urgent upper GI endoscopy after resuscitation.
  • CT angiography if ongoing major bleeding not localised or endoscopy fails.
  • Interventional radiology or surgery for uncontrolled bleeding.
04 / Management
  • Resuscitate with large-bore IV access, blood products according to haemorrhage protocol and restrictive transfusion strategy unless specific factors dictate otherwise.
  • Stop or reverse anticoagulation when clinically appropriate. Give IV acid suppression and start variceal-directed vasoactive therapy plus antibiotics when portal hypertensive bleeding is suspected.
  • Arrange urgent endoscopy after stabilisation. Escalate to interventional radiology or surgery for uncontrolled bleeding.
05 / Differentials
  • Peptic ulcer, erosive gastritis/duodenitis, varices, Mallory-Weiss tear.
  • Malignancy, Dieulafoy lesion, angiodysplasia.
  • Aortoenteric fistula after aortic graft is a must-not-miss cause.
02.03

Abdominal pain

Time-critical first
Clinical priorityProvide analgesia early while assessing. Resuscitate, keep nil by mouth and involve surgery urgently for peritonism, shock, suspected ischaemia, ruptured aneurysm, ectopic pregnancy or perforation.
01 / Bedside examination
  • A-E and identify shock, peritonism, GI bleed or ruptured ectopic pregnancy.
  • Characterise site, onset, migration, radiation, severity, meals, bowel/urinary/gynaecological symptoms and previous surgery.
  • Full abdominal exam including hernia orifices; rectal, testicular or pelvic exam when indicated.
02 / Investigations
  • FBC, UEC, LFT, lipase, CRP, lactate, glucose, urinalysis and pregnancy test.
  • Troponin/ECG for upper abdominal pain or cardiovascular risk.
  • Cultures if septic.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • Bedside ultrasound for AAA, biliary, urinary or pelvic pathology.
  • CT abdomen/pelvis for undifferentiated severe pain, obstruction, ischaemia or perforation.
  • Targeted ultrasound for biliary, pelvic or testicular disease.
04 / Management
  • Provide analgesia early while assessing. Resuscitate, keep nil by mouth and involve surgery urgently for peritonism, shock, suspected ischaemia, ruptured aneurysm, ectopic pregnancy or perforation.
  • Treat the cause with fluids, antiemetics, antibiotics when infection or perforation is suspected, and time-critical operative, endoscopic or interventional management as required.
  • Reassess serially because evolving abdominal pathology may initially have limited signs.
05 / Differentials
  • Must not miss: AAA, mesenteric ischaemia, perforation, obstruction/strangulation, ectopic pregnancy, appendicitis, pancreatitis, cholangitis.
  • Common: gastroenteritis, biliary colic, renal colic, constipation, dyspepsia.
  • Extra-abdominal: ACS, pneumonia, DKA, porphyria, shingles.
02.04

Vomiting

Time-critical first
Clinical priorityCorrect dehydration, electrolytes and glucose. Protect the airway and decompress the stomach when obstruction, ileus or severe distension is present.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Assess hydration, shock, aspiration risk, abdominal distension, peritonism and neurological signs.
  • Characterise bilious, faeculent, bloody or projectile vomiting; ask about pain, bowel function, pregnancy, drugs and toxins.
  • Abdominal, neurological and fundoscopic examination when indicated.
02 / Investigations
  • UEC, glucose, ketones, LFT, lipase, FBC, CRP and pregnancy test.
  • VBG/ABG, lactate and toxicology tests if severe or suspected poisoning.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • Abdominal X-ray/CT for obstruction or perforation.
  • Ultrasound for biliary, pelvic or pregnancy-related causes.
  • CT brain for focal deficit, raised ICP or thunderclap headache.
  • Endoscopy for obstruction, bleeding or persistent upper GI symptoms.
04 / Management
  • Correct dehydration, electrolytes and glucose. Protect the airway and decompress the stomach when obstruction, ileus or severe distension is present.
  • Give cause-appropriate antiemetic therapy and stop provoking medications.
  • Urgently treat obstruction, intracranial pathology, metabolic crisis, pregnancy complications, sepsis or surgical abdomen.
05 / Differentials
  • GI: gastroenteritis, obstruction, gastroparesis, pancreatitis, biliary disease.
  • Central: migraine, raised ICP, vestibular disease.
  • Metabolic/toxic: DKA, uraemia, adrenal crisis, drugs.
  • Pregnancy-related.
  • Must not miss: obstruction/strangulation, perforation, ectopic pregnancy, raised ICP.
02.05

Abdominal distension

Time-critical first
Clinical priorityResuscitate if pain, peritonism, sepsis, respiratory compromise or obstruction is present. Keep nil by mouth and obtain surgical review when mechanical obstruction, volvulus or perforation is possible.
01 / Bedside examination
  • A-E if pain, vomiting, sepsis or respiratory compromise.
  • Inspect contour, scars, hernias and visible peristalsis; auscultate; percuss for tympany or shifting dullness; palpate for tenderness, masses and organomegaly.
  • Assess rectum, groins and pelvic organs when indicated; measure abdominal girth only if serial trend is useful.
02 / Investigations
  • FBC, UEC, LFT, CRP, lipase, lactate and pregnancy test as appropriate.
  • Urinalysis; ascitic fluid cell count, albumin, protein and culture if new or decompensated ascites.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • Abdominal X-ray for suspected obstruction or toxic megacolon.
  • Ultrasound for ascites, liver or pelvic pathology.
  • CT abdomen/pelvis for obstruction, mass, perforation or unclear acute distension.
04 / Management
  • Resuscitate if pain, peritonism, sepsis, respiratory compromise or obstruction is present. Keep nil by mouth and obtain surgical review when mechanical obstruction, volvulus or perforation is possible.
  • Use nasogastric decompression for significant vomiting or obstruction, correct fluids and electrolytes, and treat precipitating medications or infection.
  • Manage ascites with sodium management, diuretics and therapeutic paracentesis where indicated, including albumin according to local protocol.
05 / Differentials
  • Gas: bowel obstruction, ileus, volvulus, toxic megacolon.
  • Fluid: ascites, haemoperitoneum.
  • Mass: tumour, organomegaly, pregnancy, distended bladder.
  • Other: obesity, constipation, functional bloating.
02.06

Rectal bleeding

Time-critical first
Clinical priorityResuscitate significant bleeding, quantify ongoing loss and stop or reverse anticoagulants when appropriate.
01 / Bedside examination
  • A-E and haemodynamic assessment; quantify bleeding and anticoagulant use.
  • Distinguish bright blood, maroon stool and melaena; assess pain, bowel change and weight loss.
  • Abdominal, perianal and digital rectal examination; anoscopy when appropriate.
02 / Investigations
  • FBC, UEC, LFT, coagulation, group and hold/crossmatch for significant bleeding.
  • CRP and stool studies if inflammatory/infective pattern.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • Colonoscopy after preparation for most stable lower GI bleeding.
  • CT angiography for ongoing haemodynamically significant bleeding.
  • Upper endoscopy if brisk upper source possible.
  • Anoscopy/proctoscopy for distal lesions.
04 / Management
  • Resuscitate significant bleeding, quantify ongoing loss and stop or reverse anticoagulants when appropriate.
  • Use urgent endoscopy, CT angiography, interventional radiology or surgery for haemodynamic instability or ongoing major bleeding.
  • Treat benign anorectal disease only after concerning proximal pathology has been considered; arrange colorectal investigation for persistent or unexplained bleeding.
05 / Differentials
  • Haemorrhoids, fissure, diverticular bleeding, colorectal cancer/polyps.
  • IBD, infectious or ischaemic colitis, angiodysplasia.
  • Massive upper GI bleed can present as haematochezia.
02.07

Foreign body in gastrointestinal tract

Time-critical first
Clinical priorityUrgently remove button batteries in the oesophagus, sharp objects, complete obstruction and objects causing perforation or bleeding.
01 / Bedside examination
  • A-E and assess airway, drooling, inability to swallow, chest/abdominal pain, perforation or obstruction.
  • Identify object, size, shape, number, time and whether battery, magnet, sharp or drug packet.
  • Avoid blind finger sweeps or inducing vomiting.
02 / Investigations
  • Usually no routine bloods unless complications or procedure required.
  • Toxicology testing if drug packet leakage suspected.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • Plain radiographs from neck to pelvis for radiopaque objects.
  • CT for suspected perforation, radiolucent high-risk object or uncertain location.
  • Urgent endoscopy for oesophageal obstruction, button battery, magnets, sharp objects or high-risk gastric objects.
04 / Management
  • Urgently remove button batteries in the oesophagus, sharp objects, complete obstruction and objects causing perforation or bleeding.
  • Keep nil by mouth, obtain early gastroenterology or surgical input and avoid inducing vomiting.
  • Observe selected low-risk objects with clear return precautions and interval imaging until passage is confirmed when required.
05 / Differentials
  • True ingestion versus aspiration.
  • Complications: mucosal injury, perforation, fistula, obstruction, bleeding, toxicity.
  • Mimics: food bolus, oesophageal stricture or motility disorder.
02.08

Dysphagia

Time-critical first
Clinical priorityKeep nil by mouth and obtain urgent airway or endoscopic review for complete obstruction, drooling, aspiration or food bolus impaction.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Differentiate oropharyngeal difficulty initiating swallow from oesophageal food sticking.
  • Assess solids versus liquids, progression, odynophagia, aspiration, weight loss and neurological symptoms.
  • Cranial nerves, oral cavity, neck, respiratory status and nutritional state.
02 / Investigations
  • FBC, UEC, LFT, CRP and nutritional markers when significant.
  • Autoimmune or neuromuscular tests only if clinically suggested.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • Urgent endoscopy for obstruction, food bolus, progressive symptoms or alarm features.
  • Videofluoroscopic swallow or FEES for oropharyngeal dysfunction.
  • Barium swallow and manometry for structural or motility disorders after endoscopic assessment.
  • CT neck/chest for mass or extrinsic compression.
04 / Management
  • Keep nil by mouth and obtain urgent airway or endoscopic review for complete obstruction, drooling, aspiration or food bolus impaction.
  • Perform swallow assessment and modify texture when oropharyngeal dysphagia is suspected. Provide nutrition and aspiration prevention.
  • Treat structural or inflammatory causes with endoscopic, surgical or medical therapy; suspected malignancy requires expedited referral.
05 / Differentials
  • Oropharyngeal: stroke, Parkinson disease, motor neurone disease, myasthenia, structural lesion.
  • Oesophageal mechanical: cancer, peptic stricture, ring/web, eosinophilic oesophagitis.
  • Motility: achalasia, spasm, scleroderma.
  • Must not miss: airway compromise, food bolus, malignancy.
02.09

Jaundice

Focused clinical approach
Clinical priorityTreat sepsis and suspected ascending cholangitis urgently with IV antibiotics, fluids and biliary decompression.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Confirm scleral icterus; assess fever, RUQ pain, pruritus, pale stool, dark urine, weight loss and drug/alcohol exposure.
  • Look for sepsis, chronic liver disease, hepatomegaly, palpable gallbladder, ascites and encephalopathy.
02 / Investigations
  • Fractionated bilirubin, AST/ALT, ALP/GGT, albumin, INR, FBC, UEC and lipase.
  • Haemolysis screen for unconjugated pattern.
  • Hepatitis serology, autoimmune or metabolic tests guided by pattern.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • RUQ ultrasound first for biliary dilatation and gallstones.
  • MRCP or CT for obstruction/mass.
  • ERCP for therapeutic decompression.
  • Liver Doppler, elastography or biopsy in selected hepatocellular disease.
04 / Management
  • Treat sepsis and suspected ascending cholangitis urgently with IV antibiotics, fluids and biliary decompression.
  • Stop hepatotoxic drugs, correct coagulopathy when indicated and manage pruritus, nutrition and complications of liver failure.
  • Refer obstructive or malignant causes for ERCP, surgery or oncology; acute liver failure requires urgent specialist or transplant-centre discussion.
05 / Differentials
  • Prehepatic: haemolysis, ineffective erythropoiesis.
  • Hepatocellular: viral, drug-induced, alcohol-related, autoimmune, ischaemic hepatitis.
  • Cholestatic/obstructive: stone, cholangitis, pancreatic or biliary cancer, stricture.
  • Must not miss: ascending cholangitis, acute liver failure.
02.10

Diarrhoea

Focused clinical approach
Clinical priorityAssess and replace fluid and electrolyte losses. Isolate and test promptly when healthcare-associated infection or high-consequence pathogen is possible.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Assess dehydration, sepsis, blood, mucus, nocturnal symptoms, pain, duration, travel, antibiotics, food and immunosuppression.
  • Abdominal exam; rectal exam if bleeding or severe disease.
  • Look for rash, arthritis, mouth ulcers and thyroid signs.
02 / Investigations
  • UEC and FBC if moderate/severe; CRP, lactate if toxic.
  • Stool PCR/culture for severe, bloody, persistent, outbreak or high-risk cases; C. difficile testing after antibiotic or healthcare exposure.
  • Faecal calprotectin, coeliac serology, TSH or HIV testing when indicated.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • Imaging usually unnecessary in uncomplicated acute diarrhoea.
  • CT abdomen for severe pain, toxic megacolon, ischaemia or complication.
  • Colonoscopy with biopsies for persistent inflammatory or unexplained chronic diarrhoea.
04 / Management
  • Assess and replace fluid and electrolyte losses. Isolate and test promptly when healthcare-associated infection or high-consequence pathogen is possible.
  • Use targeted antimicrobial therapy only when indicated by severity, host risk or confirmed organism. Avoid antimotility agents in severe inflammatory diarrhoea or suspected toxic megacolon.
  • Treat inflammatory, malabsorptive or medication-related causes and escalate for shock, severe colitis or organ dysfunction.
05 / Differentials
  • Acute infection, medication, toxin.
  • Inflammatory: IBD, ischaemic colitis, microscopic colitis.
  • Malabsorption, pancreatic insufficiency, bile acid diarrhoea.
  • Endocrine: hyperthyroidism, adrenal insufficiency, neuroendocrine tumour.
  • Overflow diarrhoea.
02.11

Abdominal mass

Focused clinical approach
Clinical priorityEscalate pulsatile, tender or haemodynamically significant masses urgently and avoid deep palpation if aneurysm is suspected.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Determine location, size, surface, edge, consistency, mobility, tenderness, pulsatility and movement with respiration.
  • Look for organomegaly, lymphadenopathy, ascites, hernias and systemic cancer signs.
  • Assess bladder, rectum and pelvis where relevant.
02 / Investigations
  • FBC, UEC, LFT, CRP, urinalysis and pregnancy test.
  • Tumour markers are not screening tests and should be ordered only for a defined clinical question.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • Ultrasound for initial characterisation of many masses.
  • Contrast CT abdomen/pelvis for anatomy, staging and complications.
  • MRI for liver, adrenal, pelvic or soft-tissue characterisation.
  • Image-guided biopsy only after imaging and specialist planning.
04 / Management
  • Escalate pulsatile, tender or haemodynamically significant masses urgently and avoid deep palpation if aneurysm is suspected.
  • Relieve obstruction, bleeding, infection or pain while defining the organ of origin.
  • Refer suspicious masses through the appropriate surgical or oncology pathway for staging, tissue diagnosis and multidisciplinary treatment.
05 / Differentials
  • Organ enlargement: liver, spleen, kidney, bladder, uterus.
  • Malignancy, abscess, aneurysm, cyst, faecal loading.
  • Retroperitoneal mass, lymphadenopathy, hernia.
  • Must not biopsy a possible aneurysm or catecholamine-secreting adrenal lesion before appropriate assessment.
02.12

Change in bowel habit

Focused clinical approach
Clinical priorityTreat dehydration, obstruction or severe colitis immediately when present.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Clarify new frequency, stool form, urgency, nocturnal symptoms, bleeding, mucus, pain and duration.
  • Assess weight loss, anaemia symptoms, family history, medications and diet.
  • Abdominal and rectal examination; assess lymph nodes and signs of systemic disease.
02 / Investigations
  • FBC, ferritin, CRP, UEC, LFT, TSH and coeliac serology when appropriate.
  • Stool culture/PCR, C. difficile testing or faecal calprotectin based on pattern.
  • FIT according to local colorectal pathway.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • Colonoscopy for alarm features or persistent unexplained change.
  • CT colonography if colonoscopy unsuitable.
  • CT abdomen/pelvis for mass, obstruction or systemic illness.
04 / Management
  • Treat dehydration, obstruction or severe colitis immediately when present.
  • Review diet, medications and reversible causes, then provide cause-specific constipation or diarrhoea management.
  • Fast-track investigation for iron deficiency, bleeding, weight loss, palpable mass, nocturnal symptoms or persistent unexplained change.
05 / Differentials
  • Colorectal cancer, IBD, infection, medication effect.
  • IBS, constipation with overflow, dietary change.
  • Endocrine/metabolic: thyroid disease, diabetes, hypercalcaemia.
  • Pelvic floor dysfunction.
02.13

Constipation

Focused clinical approach
Clinical priorityExclude obstruction, perforation, cauda equina syndrome, severe electrolyte disturbance and toxic megacolon before routine laxative treatment.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Define baseline and duration; assess flatus, vomiting, pain, bleeding, weight loss and medication causes.
  • Abdominal exam, hernia orifices and digital rectal exam for impaction, mass, blood and tone.
  • Neurological exam if spinal or autonomic cause suspected.
02 / Investigations
  • Usually clinical.
  • FBC, UEC, calcium, TSH and glucose for new, severe or secondary constipation.
  • Pregnancy test where relevant.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • Abdominal X-ray only for suspected obstruction, megacolon or uncertain impaction.
  • CT for obstruction, perforation or mass.
  • Colonoscopy for alarm features; transit or anorectal testing for refractory cases.
04 / Management
  • Exclude obstruction, perforation, cauda equina syndrome, severe electrolyte disturbance and toxic megacolon before routine laxative treatment.
  • Correct fluids, mobility, fibre and causative medications. Use an osmotic laxative, then add stimulant therapy according to response and local guidance.
  • Faecal impaction may require suppository, enema or manual disimpaction; investigate refractory or alarm-feature cases.
05 / Differentials
  • Functional constipation, medication-related, dehydration, low fibre or immobility.
  • Faecal impaction, colorectal cancer, stricture, volvulus.
  • Metabolic: hypercalcaemia, hypothyroidism, hypokalaemia.
  • Neurological or pelvic floor dysfunction.
02.14

Anal pain and/or tenesmus

Focused clinical approach
Clinical priorityDrain a perianal abscess promptly and obtain colorectal review for deep, recurrent or immunocompromised cases.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Inspect perianal skin, fissures, haemorrhoids, abscess, fistula and prolapse.
  • Digital rectal examination only when tolerable and not contraindicated; assess mass, blood and sphincter tone.
  • Ask about bowel habit, discharge, STI risk, IBD, immunosuppression and systemic symptoms.
02 / Investigations
  • FBC and CRP if infection or inflammatory disease.
  • STI NAAT/swabs when proctitis suspected.
  • Stool studies or faecal calprotectin for diarrhoea/IBD pattern.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • Anoscopy or proctoscopy.
  • Pelvic MRI for complex fistula or occult deep sepsis.
  • CT pelvis for acute deep abscess or systemic illness.
  • Colonoscopy for persistent tenesmus, bleeding or suspected IBD/malignancy.
04 / Management
  • Drain a perianal abscess promptly and obtain colorectal review for deep, recurrent or immunocompromised cases.
  • Treat fissure with stool softening, fibre, topical therapy and analgesia; thrombosed external haemorrhoid management depends on timing and severity.
  • Persistent tenesmus, bleeding, weight loss or a mass requires endoscopic and colorectal assessment.
05 / Differentials
  • Anal fissure, thrombosed haemorrhoid, perianal abscess, fistula.
  • Proctitis from IBD, infection or radiation.
  • Rectal cancer or faecal impaction.
  • Must not miss: Fournier gangrene, deep pelvic sepsis.
02.15

Faecal incontinence

Focused clinical approach
Clinical priorityIdentify overflow from impaction, acute neurological compression and severe diarrhoea first.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Characterise passive leakage, urgency, seepage, stool consistency and obstetric/surgical history.
  • Inspect perineum; digital rectal exam for tone, squeeze, sensation, impaction and mass.
  • Focused neurological and pelvic floor examination.
02 / Investigations
  • Stool tests if diarrhoea; FBC, UEC, TSH or coeliac tests when indicated.
  • Bladder scan if cauda equina or combined dysfunction suspected.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • Anorectal manometry and endoanal ultrasound for sphincter dysfunction.
  • Defecography for prolapse or evacuation disorder.
  • MRI spine urgently for cauda equina features.
  • Colonoscopy for bleeding or change in bowel habit.
04 / Management
  • Identify overflow from impaction, acute neurological compression and severe diarrhoea first.
  • Use bowel regulation, stool consistency optimisation, pelvic-floor physiotherapy, continence products and skin protection.
  • Refer persistent structural sphincter injury, prolapse or refractory symptoms for colorectal and continence assessment.
05 / Differentials
  • Diarrhoea or urgency, faecal impaction with overflow.
  • Obstetric or surgical sphincter injury, neuropathy, rectal prolapse.
  • Neurological disease or cauda equina.
  • Cognitive or functional impairment.
02.16

Malabsorption

Focused clinical approach
Clinical priorityReplace deficient iron, folate, B12, calcium, vitamin D and other nutrients while treating dehydration and weight loss.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Assess chronic diarrhoea, steatorrhoea, bloating, weight loss, diet, surgery and medication exposure.
  • Look for muscle wasting, oedema, bruising, glossitis, neuropathy, dermatitis herpetiformis and bone disease.
02 / Investigations
  • FBC, ferritin, B12, folate, UEC, LFT, albumin, calcium, phosphate, magnesium, INR and vitamin D.
  • Coeliac serology with total IgA; stool elastase for pancreatic insufficiency; stool infection tests if indicated.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • Upper endoscopy with duodenal biopsies for suspected coeliac disease.
  • CT/MR enterography for Crohn disease or structural disease.
  • Pancreatic imaging when insufficiency suspected.
  • Breath tests selectively for carbohydrate malabsorption or bacterial overgrowth.
04 / Management
  • Replace deficient iron, folate, B12, calcium, vitamin D and other nutrients while treating dehydration and weight loss.
  • Treat the underlying cause, such as strict gluten avoidance for coeliac disease, pancreatic enzyme replacement, infection treatment or inflammatory bowel disease therapy.
  • Use dietitian and gastroenterology input for significant weight loss, refractory symptoms or uncertain diagnosis.
05 / Differentials
  • Coeliac disease, pancreatic exocrine insufficiency, Crohn disease.
  • Post-surgical short bowel, bile acid malabsorption, small intestinal bacterial overgrowth.
  • Giardiasis, tropical sprue, medication-related disease.
  • Protein-losing enteropathy.
03.01

Oliguria / decreased urine output

Time-critical first
Clinical priorityConfirm true output and catheter patency, assess perfusion and bladder volume, and treat shock or sepsis immediately.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Confirm measurement and catheter patency; assess A-E, volume status, haemorrhage, sepsis and obstruction.
  • Review intake/output, medications and perioperative events; examine bladder and signs of fluid overload.
02 / Investigations
  • UEC/creatinine, potassium, bicarbonate, FBC, urinalysis and urine microscopy.
  • ECG for hyperkalaemia; CK or cause-specific tests when indicated.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • Bladder scan immediately.
  • Renal tract ultrasound for obstruction.
  • Bedside cardiac/lung ultrasound to guide haemodynamic assessment.
  • CT if obstruction or intra-abdominal cause unclear.
04 / Management
  • Confirm true output and catheter patency, assess perfusion and bladder volume, and treat shock or sepsis immediately.
  • Give fluid only when hypovolaemia or fluid responsiveness is likely; avoid repeated boluses in congestion.
  • Relieve obstruction and treat hyperkalaemia, acidosis or pulmonary oedema urgently; seek nephrology input if persistent.
05 / Differentials
  • Pre-renal hypoperfusion, intrinsic AKI, post-renal obstruction.
  • False oliguria from blocked/dislodged catheter or unrecorded urine.
  • Must not miss: shock, hyperkalaemia, abdominal compartment syndrome, obstructed infected system.
03.02

Acute kidney injury

Time-critical first
Clinical priorityTreat shock, sepsis and obstruction immediately. Stop nephrotoxins, adjust renally cleared medications and optimise fluid status with repeated reassessment.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Assess volume status, sepsis, haemorrhage, nephrotoxins, urine output and obstruction.
  • Examine BP, JVP, oedema, lungs, rash, joints, bladder and prostate where indicated.
  • Review baseline creatinine and medication chart; accurately measure urine output.
02 / Investigations
  • Repeat UEC/creatinine, potassium, bicarbonate, calcium, phosphate, FBC and urinalysis with microscopy.
  • Urine ACR/PCR; CK, haemolysis tests, autoimmune screen or myeloma tests when indicated.
  • ECG urgently for hyperkalaemia.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • Bladder scan and renal tract ultrasound to exclude obstruction when possible.
  • CT KUB for stones; CT abdomen for complex obstruction or mass.
  • Renal biopsy after nephrology review for unexplained intrinsic AKI.
04 / Management
  • Treat shock, sepsis and obstruction immediately. Stop nephrotoxins, adjust renally cleared medications and optimise fluid status with repeated reassessment.
  • Correct dangerous hyperkalaemia, acidosis and pulmonary oedema using emergency pathways.
  • Discuss urgently with nephrology for refractory complications, severe or rapidly progressive AKI, suspected glomerulonephritis or dialysis indications.
05 / Differentials
  • Pre-renal: hypovolaemia, sepsis, heart failure.
  • Intrinsic: ATN, glomerulonephritis, interstitial nephritis, vascular disease, rhabdomyolysis.
  • Post-renal: prostate, stone, tumour, clot or neurogenic bladder.
  • Immediate threats: hyperkalaemia, pulmonary oedema, severe acidosis, uraemic complications.
03.03

Scrotal pain

Time-critical first
Clinical priorityAssume testicular torsion until excluded. Obtain immediate urology or surgical review and do not delay exploration for imaging when clinical suspicion is high.

  • Treat acute torsion as a clinical emergency: sudden severe pain, high-riding or horizontal testis, absent cremasteric reflex, nausea.

02 / Investigations
  • Urinalysis, culture and STI NAAT when infection possible.
  • FBC/CRP if systemic illness.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • Doppler ultrasound only if it will not delay exploration and diagnosis is uncertain.
  • CT for referred abdominal/ureteric pathology when indicated.
04 / Management
  • Assume testicular torsion until excluded. Obtain immediate urology or surgical review and do not delay exploration for imaging when clinical suspicion is high.
  • Give analgesia and support. Treat epididymo-orchitis with pathogen-directed antibiotics and STI management when appropriate.
  • Urgently treat incarcerated hernia, Fournier gangrene or trauma.
05 / Differentials
  • Must not miss: testicular torsion, incarcerated hernia, Fournier gangrene.
  • Epididymo-orchitis, torsion of appendix testis, trauma, tumour haemorrhage.
  • Referred renal colic, neuropathic or chronic pelvic pain.
03.04

Difficulty passing urine / urinary retention

Time-critical first
Clinical priorityProvide prompt bladder decompression, usually with urethral catheterisation; use suprapubic access if urethral catheterisation is unsafe or unsuccessful.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Assess pain, palpable bladder, voided volumes, neurological symptoms and medication triggers.

  • Renal ultrasound for hydronephrosis or renal impairment.

  • Provide prompt bladder decompression, usually with urethral catheterisation; use suprapubic access if urethral catheterisation is unsafe or unsuccessful.

  • Outlet obstruction: BPH, prostate/urethral cancer, stricture, stone, clot.

  • Infection, constipation, postoperative retention.
03.05

Haematuria

Time-critical first
Clinical priorityResuscitate major bleeding and manage clot retention with large-bore catheterisation, irrigation and urgent urology review.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Confirm visible versus non-visible; pain, clots, infection, stone, trauma, exercise, menstruation and anticoagulation.
  • BP, abdominal/renal masses, prostate and genital examination as indicated.
  • Red urine without RBCs suggests haemoglobin, myoglobin, foods or drugs.
02 / Investigations
  • Urinalysis with microscopy, culture, UEC/eGFR, FBC and urine ACR/PCR.
  • Dysmorphic RBCs/casts suggest glomerular disease.
  • Coagulation and cytology selectively.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • CT urogram for high-risk visible haematuria; renal ultrasound in selected lower-risk or contraindicated cases.
  • Cystoscopy for visible or risk-stratified non-visible haematuria.
  • CT KUB for suspected stone.
04 / Management
  • Resuscitate major bleeding and manage clot retention with large-bore catheterisation, irrigation and urgent urology review.
  • Treat infection or stones, but do not attribute visible haematuria to these without appropriate follow-up.
  • Arrange risk-based upper-tract imaging and cystoscopy for unexplained haematuria, especially visible haematuria or malignancy risk.
05 / Differentials
  • Malignancy: bladder, renal, upper tract or prostate.
  • Infection, stones, BPH, trauma.
  • Glomerulonephritis, interstitial nephritis, papillary necrosis.
  • Anticoagulation may exacerbate but does not explain away haematuria.
03.06

Loin pain

Time-critical first
Clinical priorityProvide analgesia and antiemetics. Urgently decompress an infected obstructed collecting system and escalate suspected ruptured aneurysm or renal infarction.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Assess sudden colicky versus constant pain, fever, urinary symptoms, haematuria, trauma and pregnancy.
  • A-E if septic; renal angle, abdomen, groin, genital and spinal examination.
02 / Investigations
  • Urinalysis, pregnancy test, UEC, FBC and CRP; lactate and cultures if septic.
  • Lipase/LFT or STI testing if alternative source suspected.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • Non-contrast CT KUB for renal colic in most adults.

  • Stop causative drugs, correct electrolytes and treat rhabdomyolysis with monitored fluid therapy while avoiding overload.
  • Use physiotherapy and cause-specific neurological, endocrine, infectious or rheumatological treatment.
05 / Differentials
  • Myopathy: inflammatory, endocrine, drug/toxin, metabolic or muscular dystrophy.
  • Neuropathy, motor neurone disease, NMJ disorder.
  • Rhabdomyolysis, polymyalgia rheumatica, fibromyalgia.
  • Electrolyte disturbance or deconditioning.
05.06

Multisystem disease, vasculitis or connective tissue disorder

Focused clinical approach
Clinical priorityEscalate pulmonary haemorrhage, rapidly progressive renal failure, CNS disease, mesenteric ischaemia or severe cytopenia urgently.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Map organ involvement: constitutional, skin, joints, kidney, lung, ENT, neurological, GI and vascular.
  • Examine rash, ulcers, pulses, BP, joints, chest, nerves and urine output.
  • Identify organ-threatening disease: pulmonary haemorrhage, glomerulonephritis, CNS disease, mesenteric ischaemia or visual loss.
02 / Investigations
  • FBC, UEC/eGFR, LFT, CRP/ESR, urinalysis and urine ACR/PCR.
  • ANA with phenotype-directed antibodies, complements, ANCA, RF/anti-CCP, CK and antiphospholipid testing as indicated.
  • Hepatitis, HIV and blood cultures to exclude infectious mimics.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • CXR/CT chest, echocardiography and vascular imaging according to organs involved.
  • Biopsy the safest active site, such as skin, kidney, temporal artery or nerve/muscle, before immunosuppression when feasible.
04 / Management
  • Escalate pulmonary haemorrhage, rapidly progressive renal failure, CNS disease, mesenteric ischaemia or severe cytopenia urgently.
  • Obtain tissue diagnosis where feasible without delaying life-saving corticosteroid or immunosuppressive therapy in organ-threatening disease.
  • Coordinate rheumatology and affected specialties, provide infection prophylaxis and monitor treatment toxicity.
05 / Differentials
  • SLE, systemic sclerosis, Sjogren syndrome, inflammatory myositis, mixed connective tissue disease.
  • Small, medium or large-vessel vasculitis.
  • Mimics: infection, malignancy, drugs, embolic disease, cholesterol emboli.
05.07

Polyarthritis, 5 or more joints

Focused clinical approach
Clinical priorityEscalate systemic illness, vasculitis, severe weakness or organ involvement.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Determine inflammatory versus mechanical pattern, symmetry, small versus large joints and duration.
  • Assess systemic features: rash, ulcers, Raynaud, sicca, weakness, fever and weight loss.
  • Full joint, skin, nail, vascular and neurological examination.
02 / Investigations
  • FBC, UEC, LFT, CRP/ESR and urinalysis.
  • RF and anti-CCP for rheumatoid pattern; ANA with reflex antibodies for connective tissue phenotype.
  • Viral, hepatitis, HIV or parvovirus testing when indicated.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • Plain radiographs of symptomatic hands/feet when chronic inflammatory disease suspected.
  • Ultrasound for synovitis; MRI selectively.
  • CXR or organ imaging for systemic disease.
04 / Management
  • Escalate systemic illness, vasculitis, severe weakness or organ involvement.
  • Use analgesia and anti-inflammatory therapy while avoiding corticosteroid before infection is reasonably excluded.
  • Refer suspected inflammatory arthritis early for disease-modifying therapy and manage extra-articular risk.
05 / Differentials
  • Rheumatoid arthritis, psoriatic arthritis, SLE/connective tissue disease.
  • Viral arthritis, reactive arthritis, crystal disease.
  • Polymyalgia rheumatica in older patients.
  • Osteoarthritis can be polyarticular but is usually non-inflammatory.
05.08

Oligoarthritis, 2 to 4 joints

Focused clinical approach
Clinical priorityExclude infection in any hot joint and treat systemic sepsis promptly.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Define acute versus chronic and inflammatory features: morning stiffness, swelling and rest pain.
  • Pattern: axial/peripheral, symmetric/asymmetric, enthesitis, dactylitis, skin, eye, bowel or genitourinary symptoms.
  • Examine all joints, entheses, spine, skin, nails and eyes.
02 / Investigations
  • FBC, CRP/ESR, UEC/LFT; aspirate any hot swollen joint.
  • STI testing, stool studies or HLA-B27 only when clinically indicated.
  • RF/anti-CCP or ANA based on phenotype, not as screening panels.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • X-ray or ultrasound of affected joints.
  • MRI sacroiliac joints for suspected axial spondyloarthritis.
  • Imaging for infection or malignancy when red flags.
04 / Management
  • Exclude infection in any hot joint and treat systemic sepsis promptly.
  • Use anti-inflammatory treatment for reactive, crystal or inflammatory disease once infection is unlikely.
  • Treat the precipitating infection when indicated and refer persistent inflammatory arthritis early to rheumatology.
05 / Differentials
  • Spondyloarthritis, reactive arthritis, psoriatic arthritis.
  • Crystal disease, infection including disseminated gonococcal infection.
  • Early rheumatoid arthritis, sarcoid or IBD-associated arthritis.
  • Mechanical or traumatic disease.
05.09

Soft tissue rheumatism

Focused clinical approach
Clinical priorityRelative activity modification, ice or heat, short-course analgesia and targeted physiotherapy are first-line.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Localise tendon, enthesis, bursa, fascia or regional pain syndrome rather than joint synovitis.
  • Assess provoking movements, occupation, overuse, biomechanics and systemic inflammatory features.
  • Palpate targeted structures and test resisted movement.
02 / Investigations
  • Usually clinical with no blood tests.
  • CRP/ESR, CK or rheumatological tests only when inflammatory/systemic disease is suspected.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • Ultrasound for tendon or bursal pathology and guided injection.
  • X-ray for calcification or bony disease.
  • MRI for refractory symptoms, tear or uncertain diagnosis.
04 / Management
  • Relative activity modification, ice or heat, short-course analgesia and targeted physiotherapy are first-line.
  • Correct biomechanical and occupational contributors. Consider image-guided corticosteroid injection for selected bursitis or tendinopathy after contraindications are excluded.
  • Refer rupture, persistent weakness, atypical pain or failure of conservative therapy.
05 / Differentials
  • Tendinopathy, bursitis, enthesopathy, plantar fasciitis.
  • Rotator cuff disease, lateral epicondylalgia, greater trochanteric pain.
  • Fibromyalgia or myofascial pain.
  • Mimics: radiculopathy, inflammatory arthritis, infection or tumour.
06.01

Fit and/or blackout

Time-critical first
Clinical priorityProtect from injury, position safely, check glucose and treat status epilepticus under the local emergency pathway.
01 / Bedside examination
  • A-E during event; protect from injury, time seizure, check glucose and treat status epilepticus urgently.
  • Witness account: onset, focal signs, movements, cyanosis, tongue bite, incontinence, duration and recovery.
  • Post-event neurological, cardiovascular, injury and pregnancy assessment.
02 / Investigations
  • Bedside glucose; UEC including sodium, calcium and magnesium, FBC, LFT and pregnancy test.
  • Toxicology, antiseizure drug levels, infection tests or ABG/lactate when indicated.
  • ECG for all unexplained blackouts.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • Urgent CT brain for focal deficit, trauma, persistent altered state, immunocompromise, anticoagulation or first seizure with concern.
  • MRI and EEG for unprovoked seizure evaluation.
  • Lumbar puncture if CNS infection suspected after imaging assessment.
04 / Management
  • Protect from injury, position safely, check glucose and treat status epilepticus under the local emergency pathway.
  • Correct provoking causes such as hypoglycaemia, electrolyte disturbance, infection, toxin or withdrawal. Treat trauma and pregnancy-related causes urgently.
  • Start long-term antiseizure therapy selectively with neurology input, and provide driving, water and occupational safety advice.
05 / Differentials
  • Epileptic seizure, syncope including arrhythmia, psychogenic non-epileptic event.
  • Metabolic/toxic event, hypoglycaemia, eclampsia.
  • TIA rarely causes loss of consciousness.
  • Must not miss: status epilepticus, intracranial haemorrhage, meningitis.
06.02

Focal weakness and lesion localisation

Time-critical first
Clinical priorityActivate the stroke pathway for sudden focal deficit and establish last-known-well time. Correct glucose and avoid unnecessary delay to brain imaging.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Activate stroke pathway for sudden focal deficit; establish last known well and glucose.
  • Map distribution, tone, power, reflexes, plantar responses, sensation, cranial nerves, coordination and gait.
  • Assess fatigability, muscle bulk, fasciculations, ocular/bulbar and respiratory involvement.
02 / Investigations
  • Glucose, FBC, UEC, coagulation and other acute stroke tests.
  • CK, thyroid, B12, autoimmune or NMJ antibodies for non-acute localisation.
  • Lumbar puncture when inflammatory neuropathy suspected.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • Urgent non-contrast CT brain plus vascular imaging for acute stroke.
  • MRI brain/spine for localisation or demyelination.
  • EMG/nerve conduction and repetitive stimulation for peripheral/NMJ disease.
04 / Management
  • Activate the stroke pathway for sudden focal deficit and establish last-known-well time. Correct glucose and avoid unnecessary delay to brain imaging.
  • Use reperfusion therapy when eligible and manage haemorrhage, blood pressure and antithrombotic therapy according to stroke subtype.
  • Treat spinal cord, peripheral nerve, neuromuscular junction or muscle causes and begin early rehabilitation.
05 / Differentials
  • UMN: increased tone, hyperreflexia, clonus, extensor plantar response, pyramidal pattern; brain or spinal cord lesion.
  • LMN: reduced tone/reflexes, wasting, fasciculations; anterior horn, root, plexus or nerve.
  • NMJ: fluctuating fatigable weakness, normal sensation, reflexes often preserved; myasthenia or botulism.

06.03

Raised intracranial pressure

Time-critical first
Clinical priorityUrgently involve ICU, neurosurgery and neurology. Elevate the head, maintain oxygenation and perfusion, avoid hypotonic fluid and prevent fever or seizures.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Emergency: headache, vomiting, reduced consciousness, papilloedema, sixth nerve palsy, seizures or Cushing response.
  • A-E with head elevated; avoid hypotension and hypoxia; urgent neurosurgical discussion.
  • Focused neurological exam and fundoscopy without delaying imaging.
02 / Investigations
  • FBC, UEC, coagulation, glucose and cause-specific infection/endocrine tests.
  • Avoid lumbar puncture until mass effect and obstruction are excluded.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • Urgent non-contrast CT brain; CT angiography/venography as indicated.
  • MRI for tumour, posterior fossa, venous thrombosis or inflammatory disease.
  • ICP monitoring in selected ICU/neurosurgical patients.
04 / Management
  • Urgently involve ICU, neurosurgery and neurology. Elevate the head, maintain oxygenation and perfusion, avoid hypotonic fluid and prevent fever or seizures.
  • Use hyperosmolar therapy and controlled ventilation only in appropriate impending-herniation or monitored settings.
  • Treat the cause with surgery, CSF diversion, antimicrobials, oncological therapy or venous thrombosis treatment.
05 / Differentials
  • Mass lesion, haemorrhage, hydrocephalus, cerebral oedema, venous sinus thrombosis.
  • Meningitis/encephalitis, idiopathic intracranial hypertension.
  • Must not miss impending herniation.
06.04

Headache

Time-critical first
Clinical priorityThunderclap headache, meningism, focal deficit, pregnancy hypertension, raised ICP or temporal arteritis requires urgent targeted treatment and imaging.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Red flags: sudden thunderclap, fever/meningism, focal deficit, altered state, papilloedema, pregnancy/postpartum, cancer/immunosuppression, age over 50 with new headache or exertional/Valsalva onset.
  • Full neurological exam, BP, fundoscopy, temporal arteries and neck examination.
  • Characterise phenotype and medication overuse.
02 / Investigations
  • Usually none for typical primary headache.
  • FBC/CRP/ESR for infection or giant cell arteritis; pregnancy test and cause-specific tests.
  • Lumbar puncture for meningitis or suspected SAH after appropriate imaging.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • Urgent CT brain for thunderclap, trauma or acute neurological concern.
  • CT angiography for SAH/dissection; MRI for subacute focal, posterior fossa or pressure-related concern.
  • Venography for cerebral venous sinus thrombosis risk.
04 / Management
  • Thunderclap headache, meningism, focal deficit, pregnancy hypertension, raised ICP or temporal arteritis requires urgent targeted treatment and imaging.
  • Give appropriate analgesia and antiemetic therapy while avoiding opioids for routine primary headache.
  • Treat migraine or cluster headache specifically and establish preventive therapy when attacks are frequent or disabling.
05 / Differentials
  • Primary: migraine, tension-type, cluster and other trigeminal autonomic headache.
  • Must not miss: SAH, meningitis, intracranial haemorrhage, mass, venous sinus thrombosis, GCA, acute glaucoma, pre-eclampsia.
  • Medication overuse and cervicogenic headache.
06.05

Space-occupying lesion in the brain

Time-critical first
Clinical priorityEscalate seizures, focal deficit, hydrocephalus, haemorrhage or raised ICP urgently. Give corticosteroid for symptomatic vasogenic oedema from tumour when appropriate, but not routinely for all lesions.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Assess raised ICP, seizures, focal deficits, cognitive/personality change and cancer/infection history.
  • Full neurological exam including visual fields, fundoscopy and endocrine signs for sellar lesions.
  • Deterioration or herniation features require immediate escalation.
02 / Investigations
  • FBC, UEC, LFT, coagulation and infection/immunosuppression-directed tests.
  • Endocrine profile for sellar lesion; tumour markers rarely diagnostic.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • MRI brain with contrast is preferred.
  • CT for emergency haemorrhage/mass effect or when MRI unavailable.
  • Systemic CT/PET for primary cancer search when metastasis suspected.
  • Biopsy/resection planned by neurosurgery and oncology.
04 / Management
  • Escalate seizures, focal deficit, hydrocephalus, haemorrhage or raised ICP urgently. Give corticosteroid for symptomatic vasogenic oedema from tumour when appropriate, but not routinely for all lesions.
  • Use antiseizure treatment for seizures rather than universal prophylaxis.
  • Obtain neurosurgical and oncology input for biopsy, resection, radiotherapy or systemic therapy; treat abscess with antibiotics and drainage.
05 / Differentials
  • Primary tumour: glioma, meningioma, pituitary tumour.
  • Metastasis, abscess, granuloma, lymphoma.
  • Haematoma, demyelination, infarct with swelling.
  • Cystic or congenital lesion.
06.06

Brain death, neurological determination of death

Time-critical first
Clinical priorityContinue physiological support while confirming irreversibility, excluding confounders and meeting all legal and institutional prerequisites.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Specialist-led legal process. Confirm catastrophic irreversible brain injury and prerequisites before testing.
  • Exclude confounders: hypothermia, sedatives/paralytics, severe metabolic/endocrine disturbance, shock and residual drug effect.
  • Clinical examination demonstrates unresponsive coma, absent brainstem reflexes and no spontaneous breathing on a formal apnoea test.
02 / Investigations
  • Drug levels, electrolytes, glucose, acid-base status and endocrine tests as needed to exclude confounders.
  • Document core temperature, blood pressure, oxygenation and neuromuscular function prerequisites.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • Ancillary cerebral perfusion testing is used when the clinical examination or apnoea test cannot be safely or completely performed, according to jurisdiction and ANZICS/local policy.
  • CT/MRI may establish cause but does not itself determine brain death.
04 / Management
  • Continue physiological support while confirming irreversibility, excluding confounders and meeting all legal and institutional prerequisites.
  • Use the formal neurological determination protocol, including required clinical examinations and apnoea testing or ancillary testing when indicated.
  • Communicate clearly with family, document precisely and involve donation services separately from the determination process.
05 / Differentials
  • Mimics/confounders: sedative or neuromuscular blockade, hypothermia, locked-in syndrome, severe metabolic coma, high cervical cord injury.
  • Brain death is distinct from coma, vegetative state and circulatory death.
06.07

Vertigo and dizziness

Focused clinical approach
Clinical priorityTreat as possible posterior circulation stroke when acute continuous vertigo is accompanied by focal signs, severe gait ataxia, new headache or high vascular risk.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Clarify vertigo, presyncope, disequilibrium or nonspecific dizziness.
  • For acute vestibular syndrome assess gait, nystagmus and ocular alignment; HINTS only by trained clinicians in continuous vertigo with spontaneous nystagmus.
  • Full neurological, ear and cardiovascular exam; orthostatic vitals.
02 / Investigations
  • Bedside glucose and ECG when appropriate.
  • FBC, UEC and cause-specific tests rather than routine panels.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • Urgent stroke imaging for focal signs, inability to walk, central nystagmus or high suspicion.
  • MRI is more sensitive for posterior circulation infarct but can be early false-negative.
  • Audiology or vestibular testing for persistent peripheral symptoms.
04 / Management
  • Treat as possible posterior circulation stroke when acute continuous vertigo is accompanied by focal signs, severe gait ataxia, new headache or high vascular risk.
  • Use repositioning manoeuvres for confirmed BPPV and short-term vestibular suppressants only for severe acute symptoms.
  • Treat vestibular neuritis, migraine, orthostatic or cardiac causes and provide vestibular rehabilitation for persistent imbalance.
05 / Differentials
  • Peripheral: BPPV, vestibular neuritis, Meniere disease, labyrinthitis.
  • Central: posterior circulation stroke, demyelination, tumour, migraine.
  • Non-vertiginous: orthostatic hypotension, arrhythmia, medication, anxiety, sensory impairment.
06.08

Repeated falls

Focused clinical approach
Clinical priorityTreat injury, syncope, stroke, infection, delirium and medication toxicity first.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Establish whether fall, syncope or seizure; circumstances, prodrome, injuries and ability to rise.
  • Orthostatic vitals, vision, gait, balance, lower limb power, sensation, feet, joints, cognition and cardiac rhythm.
  • Review medications, continence, environment and alcohol.
02 / Investigations
  • ECG, FBC, UEC, glucose, calcium, B12 and TSH when indicated.
  • Urinalysis only with urinary symptoms; avoid attributing falls to asymptomatic bacteriuria.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • Imaging for injury or focal neurological signs.
  • CT/MRI brain for new focal deficit, head trauma or suspected central disease.
  • Cardiac monitoring/echo for suspected syncope.
04 / Management
  • Treat injury, syncope, stroke, infection, delirium and medication toxicity first.
  • Use multifactorial intervention: medication reduction, strength and balance training, vision and footwear correction, home modification and management of postural hypotension.
  • Address osteoporosis and fracture prevention, and arrange geriatric or falls-clinic review for recurrent unexplained falls.
05 / Differentials
  • Multifactorial frailty, medication, orthostatic hypotension, gait or vision impairment.
  • Parkinsonism, neuropathy, stroke, cerebellar disease.
  • Arrhythmia/syncope, seizure.
  • Environmental hazards, intoxication or non-accidental injury.
06.09

Numbness and tingling

Focused clinical approach

  • Define distribution, onset, progression, positive versus negative symptoms and associated weakness or sphincter dysfunction.

  • Glucose/HbA1c, B12/folate, TSH, UEC, calcium, magnesium, FBC and SPEP when peripheral neuropathy suspected.

  • Escalate sudden unilateral symptoms, spinal cord signs, cauda equina features, respiratory weakness or rapidly ascending sensory loss.

  • Stroke/TIA, spinal cord compression, cauda equina.

07.01

Burn

Time-critical first
Clinical priorityStop the burning process, cool with running water for the recommended period when timely, prevent hypothermia and assess inhalation injury early.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Stop burning process, A-E, consider inhalation injury and carbon monoxide toxicity.
  • Estimate total body surface area, depth, site, circumferential injury and associated trauma.
  • Check distal perfusion and temperature; remove jewellery and constricting items.
02 / Investigations
  • Carboxyhaemoglobin and ABG/VBG for smoke exposure; lactate for severe fire exposure/cyanide concern.
  • FBC, UEC, glucose, coagulation and group/crossmatch for major burns.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • CXR and bronchoscopy for suspected inhalation injury.
  • Trauma imaging as indicated.
  • Burn imaging is otherwise clinical; serial photos can document progression according to policy.

  • Stop the burning process, cool with running water for the recommended period when timely, prevent hypothermia and assess inhalation injury early.

05 / Differentials
  • Thermal, chemical, electrical, radiation and friction burns.
  • Complications: airway oedema, inhalation injury, shock, compartment syndrome, hypothermia, infection.

07.02

Bite

Time-critical first
Clinical priorityIrrigate thoroughly, debride devitalised tissue and assess tendon, joint, nerve and vascular injury.
01 / Bedside examination
  • A-E for major trauma, envenomation or anaphylaxis.
  • Identify animal/human, time, site, depth, crush injury, contamination and rabies/tetanus risk.
  • Neurovascular, tendon, joint and bone examination; mark erythema and assess systemic infection.
02 / Investigations
  • Usually clinical.

03 / Imaging and procedures
  • X-ray for tooth/foreign body, fracture or joint involvement.

  • Irrigate thoroughly, debride devitalised tissue and assess tendon, joint, nerve and vascular injury.

  • Local traumatic injury and infection including Pasteurella, Capnocytophaga, staphylococci, streptococci and human oral flora.

  • Melanoma, SCC, BCC, Merkel cell tumour.

  • Ulcer: vascular, neuropathic, pressure, inflammatory, infection or malignancy.

Sore throat

Time-critical first
Clinical prioritySecure the airway and involve ENT or anaesthetics urgently for drooling, stridor, trismus, muffled voice, neck swelling or deep-space infection.

  • A-E and assess drooling, trismus, muffled voice, stridor and neck swelling.

  • Rapid antigen/culture according to local streptococcal pathway.
  • FBC, EBV testing, HIV/STI testing when indicated.

  • Secure the airway and involve ENT or anaesthetics urgently for drooling, stridor, trismus, muffled voice, neck swelling or deep-space infection.

08.03

Epistaxis

Time-critical first
Clinical prioritySit forward, pinch the soft nose continuously, suction clots and resuscitate significant bleeding. Correct severe hypertension only when clinically required and reverse anticoagulation selectively.
01 / Bedside examination
  • A-E, sit forward, suction, direct pressure to soft nose and assess haemodynamic loss.
  • Identify anterior versus posterior source, anticoagulants, trauma, hypertension and bleeding disorder.
  • Examine oral cavity for posterior bleeding; avoid repeated traumatic inspection.
02 / Investigations
  • None for minor resolved bleed.
  • FBC, coagulation, UEC and group/crossmatch for major, recurrent or anticoagulated bleeding.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • Anterior rhinoscopy or nasal endoscopy.
  • CT/angiography only for trauma, mass or refractory bleeding.
  • Interventional angiography for embolisation in uncontrolled cases.
04 / Management
  • Sit forward, pinch the soft nose continuously, suction clots and resuscitate significant bleeding. Correct severe hypertension only when clinically required and reverse anticoagulation selectively.
  • Use topical vasoconstrictor, cautery or anterior packing for visible anterior bleeding.
  • Posterior, recurrent or uncontrolled bleeding requires ENT, endoscopic control or arterial embolisation.
05 / Differentials
  • Anterior septal bleeding, trauma/dryness, medication or coagulopathy.
  • Posterior arterial bleed, hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia.
  • Tumour, foreign body or invasive infection.
08.04

Hearing loss

Time-critical first
Clinical prioritySudden sensorineural hearing loss is an otological emergency. Arrange urgent audiology and ENT assessment and commence corticosteroid therapy when appropriate.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Determine sudden versus gradual, unilateral versus bilateral, conductive versus sensorineural.
  • Otoscopy, whisper/finger rub and Weber/Rinne testing; cranial nerves and vestibular exam.
  • Sudden sensorineural hearing loss is an otological emergency.
02 / Investigations
  • Usually no routine blood tests.
  • Autoimmune, infection or metabolic tests only for selected sensorineural patterns.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • Formal audiogram and tympanometry.
  • MRI internal auditory canals for asymmetric sensorineural loss or neurological signs.
  • CT temporal bone for conductive structural disease or trauma.
04 / Management
  • Sudden sensorineural hearing loss is an otological emergency. Arrange urgent audiology and ENT assessment and commence corticosteroid therapy when appropriate.
  • Remove wax or treat middle-ear disease when conductive loss is confirmed.
  • Provide hearing rehabilitation, hearing aids or implant assessment for persistent loss and address ototoxic exposure.
05 / Differentials
  • Conductive: wax, otitis media, perforation, otosclerosis, cholesteatoma.
  • Sensorineural: presbycusis, noise, sudden idiopathic loss, ototoxicity, vestibular schwannoma.
  • Mixed loss.

Hoarseness

08.06

Ear pain and/or discharge

Focused clinical approach
Clinical priorityTreat mastoiditis, malignant otitis externa, facial weakness, intracranial complication or severe systemic illness urgently.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Inspect pinna, mastoid and canal; otoscopy of both ears; assess cranial nerves and hearing.
  • Ask about swimming, trauma, diabetes/immunosuppression, fever, vertigo and referred dental/throat pain.
  • Severe nocturnal pain with granulation in a diabetic suggests necrotising otitis externa.
02 / Investigations
  • Usually clinical.
  • Swab persistent/refractory discharge; FBC/CRP and cultures if invasive infection suspected.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • CT temporal bone for mastoiditis, invasive infection, cholesteatoma complication or trauma.
  • Audiology after acute infection if hearing loss persists.
04 / Management
  • Treat mastoiditis, malignant otitis externa, facial weakness, intracranial complication or severe systemic illness urgently.

  • Keep perforated ears dry, avoid ototoxic drops when the tympanic membrane is not intact and arrange ENT follow-up for persistent discharge.
05 / Differentials
  • Otitis externa, acute otitis media with perforation, chronic suppurative otitis media.
  • Mastoiditis, cholesteatoma, foreign body.
  • Referred pain from teeth, TMJ, pharynx or head and neck cancer.
08.07

Rhinorrhoea

01 / Bedside examination
  • Differentiate clear, purulent, bloody and unilateral discharge; assess allergy, infection, trauma and foreign body.

  • Usually none.

  • Treat severe infection, orbital complication or suspected CSF leak urgently and advise avoidance of nose blowing with a possible CSF leak.

  • Viral rhinitis, allergic/non-allergic rhinitis, sinusitis.
  • Foreign body, medication-related rhinitis.
  • CSF leak, tumour or polyp.
08.08

Tinnitus

Focused clinical approach
Clinical priorityUrgently assess pulsatile tinnitus, sudden hearing loss, focal neurological signs or unilateral persistent symptoms.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Characterise unilateral/bilateral, pulsatile/non-pulsatile, hearing loss, vertigo, neurological signs and medication exposure.
  • Otoscopy, hearing tests, cranial nerves and auscultation around ear/neck for bruit.
  • Sudden hearing loss or focal neurological symptoms require urgent referral.
02 / Investigations
  • Usually none.
  • FBC, TSH or other tests only when a systemic cause is suspected.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • Audiogram for persistent tinnitus.
  • MRI internal auditory canals for unilateral/asymmetric non-pulsatile tinnitus with hearing loss.
  • CT/MR angiography/venography for pulsatile tinnitus.
04 / Management
  • Urgently assess pulsatile tinnitus, sudden hearing loss, focal neurological signs or unilateral persistent symptoms.
  • Treat reversible ear disease and review ototoxic drugs and noise exposure.
  • Use education, hearing rehabilitation and tinnitus-focused cognitive behavioural therapy or sound strategies for chronic bothersome tinnitus.
05 / Differentials
  • Sensorineural hearing loss, noise exposure, ototoxic drugs, ear disease.

09.01

Sepsis

Time-critical first
Clinical priorityRecognise, resuscitate and refer: A-E assessment, monitoring, IV access, lactate, cultures and early senior escalation.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Recognise infection plus acute organ dysfunction; A-E, full observations, mental state, urine output and perfusion.
  • Activate local sepsis and deterioration pathway; obtain senior/critical care support.
  • Search for source while resuscitating and reassess after every intervention.
02 / Investigations
  • Lactate, blood gas, FBC, UEC, LFT, coagulation, glucose and blood cultures.
  • Source-specific cultures; repeat lactate and organ function tests based on response.
  • Antibiotics must not be delayed by difficult cultures.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • CXR and bedside ultrasound early; CT or ultrasound to locate source when stable enough.
  • Urgent procedural/surgical source control for obstruction, abscess, leak, infected device or necrotic tissue.
04 / Management
  • Recognise, resuscitate and refer: A-E assessment, monitoring, IV access, lactate, cultures and early senior escalation.
  • Give appropriate IV antimicrobials promptly, balanced crystalloid in reassessed boluses for hypoperfusion, and norepinephrine for persistent hypotension with a usual MAP target of at least 65 mmHg.
  • Identify and control the source urgently, monitor urine output and lactate trend, and involve ICU when shock or organ dysfunction persists.
05 / Differentials
  • Source syndromes: pneumonia, urinary, abdominal/pelvic, skin/soft tissue, line/device, CNS and endocarditis.
  • Mimics: haemorrhage, PE, pancreatitis, adrenal crisis, anaphylaxis, thyroid storm, drug reaction.
  • Use current NSW CEC Adult Sepsis Pathway and local antimicrobial policy.
09.02

Febrile neutropenia / immunosuppressed patient with fever

Time-critical first
Clinical priorityTreat as a medical emergency. Obtain cultures and give empiric anti-pseudomonal IV antibiotics promptly under the local febrile neutropenia pathway.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Oncological emergency: A-E, sepsis screen, immediate senior and haematology/oncology involvement.
  • Do not rely on pus, leukocytosis or localising signs; examine mouth, lungs, abdomen, perineum, skin, line and wounds.
  • Avoid rectal examination, suppositories and traumatic procedures in severe neutropenia.
02 / Investigations
  • FBC with differential, UEC, LFT, lactate and at least two blood culture sets including line cultures when present.
  • Urine, respiratory, stool or lesion specimens based on symptoms.
  • Do not delay empiric antipseudomonal antibiotics for investigations.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • CXR for respiratory symptoms; CT chest may detect occult infection.
  • CT abdomen/pelvis for abdominal/perineal symptoms or persistent fever.
  • Further fungal imaging/tests for prolonged neutropenia or persistent fever.
04 / Management
  • Treat as a medical emergency. Obtain cultures and give empiric anti-pseudomonal IV antibiotics promptly under the local febrile neutropenia pathway.
  • Resuscitate sepsis, avoid rectal examination or procedures, and use protective precautions where indicated.
  • Involve haematology or oncology early, add antifungal or resistant-organism coverage based on duration, focus and clinical course, and use G-CSF selectively.
05 / Differentials
  • Bacterial sepsis including Gram-negative and line-related infection.
  • Invasive fungal or viral infection.
  • Drug fever, transfusion reaction, tumour fever or thrombosis.
  • Neutropenic enterocolitis is a must-not-miss cause of abdominal pain.
09.03

Fever in the perioperative or hospital-acquired setting

Time-critical first
Clinical priorityAssess A-E and activate the sepsis pathway for organ dysfunction. Review the operation, devices, medications, transfusions and timing.
01 / Bedside examination
  • A-E and review operation, timing, devices, antibiotics and observation trend.
  • Examine lungs, wound, abdomen, drains, IV/central lines, urine catheter, calves and pressure areas.
  • Look for anastomotic leak, deep collection, necrotising infection and non-infectious deterioration.
02 / Investigations
  • FBC, UEC, LFT, CRP and lactate if unwell.
  • Blood cultures for sepsis/rigors; urine culture only when clinically suspected; targeted respiratory and wound/deep cultures.
  • Review drug and transfusion history.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • CXR for respiratory features.
  • CT abdomen/pelvis with contrast for suspected leak or abscess.
  • Ultrasound for DVT or collection; line imaging/removal as indicated.
04 / Management
  • Assess A-E and activate the sepsis pathway for organ dysfunction. Review the operation, devices, medications, transfusions and timing.
  • Obtain targeted cultures and imaging, start empiric antibiotics when infection is likely, and remove or replace infected lines or catheters.
  • Prioritise source control for anastomotic leak, abscess, wound infection or obstructed system; also treat PE, drug reaction and non-infectious causes.
05 / Differentials
  • Common: UTI, pneumonia, wound infection, line infection, DVT/PE, anastomotic leak and abscess.
  • Other: drug fever, transfusion reaction, pancreatitis, haematoma, gout, C. difficile.
  • Early postoperative fever can be inflammatory, but instability must never be dismissed.
09.04

Fever in the returned traveller

Time-critical first
Clinical priorityIsolate when a high-consequence infection is possible and seek infectious diseases or public-health advice early.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Immediate isolation and infection-control assessment based on destination and syndrome.
  • Detailed itinerary, dates, incubation, mosquito/freshwater/animal/food/sexual exposure, prophylaxis and vaccines.
  • A-E; examine rash, jaundice, bleeding, hepatosplenomegaly, lymph nodes and neurological state.
02 / Investigations
  • Malaria testing urgently for any febrile traveller from an endemic area, with repeat testing if suspicion persists.
  • FBC, UEC, LFT, CRP, blood cultures and urinalysis.
  • Dengue/arbovirus, enteric fever, HIV, hepatitis and stool testing according to exposure and incubation.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • CXR or source-directed imaging.
  • Ultrasound for hepatobiliary or splenic disease; CT for focal complications.
  • Notify public health and involve infectious diseases early for high-consequence pathogens.
04 / Management
  • Isolate when a high-consequence infection is possible and seek infectious diseases or public-health advice early.
  • Exclude malaria urgently in any febrile traveller from an endemic area, repeating testing when clinically indicated.
  • Resuscitate sepsis and use exposure-directed treatment for malaria, enteric fever, dengue complications, rickettsial disease or diarrhoeal infection.
05 / Differentials
  • Must not miss: malaria, severe dengue, typhoid/enteric fever, meningococcaemia, viral haemorrhagic fever based on exposure.
  • Common: diarrhoeal illness, respiratory viral infection, hepatitis, rickettsial disease.
  • Non-infectious: VTE, drug reaction.
09.05

Common bacterial infections

Focused clinical approach
Clinical priorityAssess severity, source, host risk and need for source control before choosing antibiotics.
01 / Bedside examination
  • A-E and screen for sepsis or organ dysfunction.
  • Localise source: respiratory, urinary, skin/soft tissue, abdominal, line/device, CNS, bone/joint or endocardial.
  • Assess host risk: age, pregnancy, diabetes, immunosuppression, recent hospitalisation, antibiotics and resistant organism history.
02 / Investigations
  • Source-directed specimens before antibiotics when this does not delay care.
  • FBC, UEC, LFT, CRP and lactate for systemic illness; blood cultures for sepsis, endovascular infection or rigors.
  • Interpret colonisation and contamination carefully.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • Source-directed imaging: CXR, ultrasound, CT, MRI or echocardiography.
  • Drainage, aspiration or tissue sampling often provides the best microbiology and source control.
04 / Management
  • Assess severity, source, host risk and need for source control before choosing antibiotics.
  • Obtain appropriate cultures without delaying treatment in severe infection, then use empiric therapy based on local guidelines and narrow promptly.
  • Drain collections, remove infected devices and review clinical response, renal dosing and planned duration daily.
05 / Differentials
  • Respiratory: pneumococcus, H. influenzae, staphylococci and Gram-negatives by setting.
  • Urinary/intra-abdominal: Enterobacterales, enterococci and anaerobes by source.
  • Skin: staphylococci and streptococci.
  • Always consider non-bacterial mimics and local resistance patterns.
09.06

Recurrent infections

Focused clinical approach
Clinical priorityTreat each acute infection appropriately and obtain cultures before repeated empirical courses when possible.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Define site, organism, severity, frequency, response and age of onset.
  • Assess anatomical obstruction, skin barrier, aspiration, diabetes, medications, HIV risk and family history.
  • Examine growth/nutrition, nodes, spleen, skin, oral cavity and chronic lung disease.
02 / Investigations
  • FBC with differential and film, glucose/HbA1c, HIV test, quantitative immunoglobulins.
  • Complement, lymphocyte subsets, vaccine antibody responses or neutrophil function after immunology review.
  • Culture documented episodes rather than assuming recurrence.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • Source-directed imaging for bronchiectasis, sinus disease, urinary obstruction or abscess.
  • Endoscopy or anatomical studies when local structural cause suspected.
04 / Management
  • Treat each acute infection appropriately and obtain cultures before repeated empirical courses when possible.
  • Correct anatomical obstruction, poor glycaemic control, malnutrition, skin-barrier issues and medication-related immunosuppression.
  • Refer for immunology, infectious diseases or specialty assessment when infections are severe, unusual, opportunistic or associated with poor growth, cytopenia or family history.
05 / Differentials
  • Common acquired causes: exposure, chronic lung disease, diabetes, malnutrition, medications and HIV.
  • Anatomical obstruction or foreign body.
  • Primary antibody, complement, phagocyte or T-cell disorder.
  • Misdiagnosed inflammatory or non-infective disease.
09.07

Fever of unknown origin

Focused clinical approach
Clinical priorityAvoid blind prolonged antibiotics in a stable patient because they obscure diagnosis, except when neutropenic, critically ill or a specific indication exists.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Confirm documented fever and duration; repeat detailed history and examination rather than ordering indiscriminate panels.
  • Review travel, drugs, devices, procedures, immunosuppression, exposures and factitious fever risk.
  • Re-examine skin, mouth, nodes, heart, temporal arteries, joints, abdomen and retina.
02 / Investigations
  • FBC/film, UEC, LFT, CRP/ESR, urinalysis, multiple blood cultures, HIV and other targeted microbiology.
  • Autoimmune and malignancy tests guided by clues; review prior data and medication timeline.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • CXR and abdominal ultrasound or CT as initial structural assessment.
  • Echocardiography for endocarditis risk.
  • PET-CT, temporal artery ultrasound/biopsy or tissue biopsy in selected persistent cases.
04 / Management
  • Avoid blind prolonged antibiotics in a stable patient because they obscure diagnosis, except when neutropenic, critically ill or a specific indication exists.
  • Repeat history and examination, remove unnecessary devices and pursue focused tests or biopsy based on emerging clues.
  • Treat the identified infectious, inflammatory, malignant or drug cause with multidisciplinary input.
05 / Differentials
  • Infection: endocarditis, TB, occult abscess.
  • Inflammatory: vasculitis, connective tissue disease, adult-onset Still disease.
  • Malignancy: lymphoma, renal cell carcinoma and others.
  • Drugs, thrombosis, thyroiditis and factitious fever.
10.01

Bleeding disorder

Time-critical first
Clinical priorityControl active bleeding with pressure, procedural source control and major haemorrhage activation when required.
01 / Bedside examination
  • A-E and control active bleeding; assess haemodynamic compromise and intracranial/retroperitoneal sites.
  • History: mucocutaneous versus deep tissue bleeding, lifelong versus acquired, family history, liver/renal disease and medications.
  • Examine petechiae, purpura, joints, mucosa, liver, spleen and lymph nodes.
02 / Investigations
  • FBC/film, PT/INR, APTT, fibrinogen, UEC, LFT and group/crossmatch when significant.
  • Mixing studies, von Willebrand testing, factor assays, platelet function or DIC tests after haematology input.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • CT head for neurological symptoms or trauma; CT abdomen/pelvis for occult major bleeding.
  • Ultrasound for joint/soft-tissue bleeding; endoscopy or angiography by source.
04 / Management
  • Control active bleeding with pressure, procedural source control and major haemorrhage activation when required.
  • Stop and reverse anticoagulants or antiplatelets selectively, and replace specific deficiencies with targeted blood products or factor concentrates guided by clinical context and haematology.
  • Avoid unnecessary intramuscular injections or invasive procedures and treat the underlying hepatic, immune, inherited or consumptive disorder.
05 / Differentials
  • Platelet number/function disorders, von Willebrand disease.
  • Coagulation factor deficiency or inhibitor, anticoagulants.
  • Liver disease, DIC, uraemia, massive transfusion.
  • Vascular/connective tissue fragility.
10.02

Pancytopenia

Time-critical first
Clinical priorityStabilise sepsis, bleeding and symptomatic anaemia. Use neutropenic precautions and urgent antibiotics for fever.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Assess bleeding, infection, anaemia symptoms, medications, toxins, alcohol, infection and cancer treatment.
  • Examine pallor, petechiae, nodes, liver/spleen, bone tenderness and neurological signs.
02 / Investigations
  • Repeat FBC, reticulocytes and blood film urgently.
  • UEC, LFT, LDH, bilirubin, haptoglobin, B12/folate, iron, coagulation and infection tests.

03 / Imaging and procedures
  • Ultrasound for splenomegaly.
  • Bone marrow aspirate/biopsy after haematology review.
  • CT for lymphadenopathy or malignancy when indicated.
04 / Management
  • Stabilise sepsis, bleeding and symptomatic anaemia. Use neutropenic precautions and urgent antibiotics for fever.
  • Stop marrow-toxic drugs and transfuse red cells or platelets according to symptoms, bleeding risk and local thresholds.
  • Obtain urgent haematology review for marrow examination and cause-specific treatment, including leukaemia, aplasia, deficiency or hypersplenism.

  • Marrow failure: aplastic anaemia, MDS, acute leukaemia, infiltration.
  • Megaloblastic deficiency, drugs/toxins, infection.
  • Peripheral destruction/sequestration: hypersplenism, DIC, autoimmune disease.
  • Haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis.
10.03

Raised red cell or white cell count

Time-critical first
Clinical priorityHyperleukocytosis with respiratory or neurological symptoms requires urgent haematology input, cytoreduction and tumour-lysis prevention.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Confirm persistence and review dehydration, smoking, hypoxia, infection, steroids and symptoms of myeloproliferative disease.

  • Repeat FBC with differential and film.

  • Red cells: relative haemoconcentration, hypoxic secondary erythrocytosis, EPO-producing tumour, polycythaemia vera.
  • White cells: infection/inflammation, stress/steroids, smoking, leukaemia or myeloproliferative neoplasm.

10.04

Lymphadenopathy

Focused clinical approach
Clinical priorityTreat airway compromise, sepsis or rapidly progressive compressive symptoms urgently.

  • Document site, size, tenderness, mobility, consistency and generalised versus localised pattern.

02 / Investigations
  • FBC/film, CRP/ESR, LFT, LDH and targeted EBV/CMV/HIV/TB testing.

  • Treat airway compromise, sepsis or rapidly progressive compressive symptoms urgently.

  • Reactive bacterial/viral infection.
  • Lymphoma, leukaemia, metastatic malignancy.
  • TB or atypical infection.
  • Sarcoidosis, SLE and medication-related lymphadenopathy.
10.05

Splenomegaly

Focused clinical approach
Clinical priorityTreat rupture, haemodynamic instability or severe pain as an emergency and avoid palpation or contact activity when rupture risk is high.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Confirm palpable spleen, assess tenderness and measure below costal margin; distinguish from kidney.

  • Acute LUQ pain or shock suggests infarct or rupture.
02 / Investigations
  • FBC/film, reticulocytes, haemolysis screen, LFT, CRP/ESR and targeted infection tests.

03 / Imaging and procedures
  • Ultrasound confirms size and portal flow.
  • CT for focal lesion, infarct, rupture, lymphadenopathy or malignancy.

  • Treat rupture, haemodynamic instability or severe pain as an emergency and avoid palpation or contact activity when rupture risk is high.
  • Treat the underlying infection, portal hypertension, haemolysis or haematological disorder.

  • Portal hypertension/liver disease.
  • Haematological malignancy or myeloproliferative disease.
  • Haemolytic anaemia, infection including EBV/malaria.
  • Storage/infiltrative disease, splenic vein thrombosis.
11.01

Spinal cord compression

Time-critical first
Clinical priorityTreat as an oncological emergency. Keep spinal precautions when instability is possible, give corticosteroid promptly when malignant compression is suspected and obtain urgent whole-spine MRI.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Oncological and neurological emergency: new severe back pain, nocturnal/mechanical pain, weakness, sensory level or bladder/bowel dysfunction.
  • Urgent full neurological exam including gait if safe, reflexes, perianal sensation and sphincter function.
  • Immobilise if instability suspected and involve oncology/neurosurgery immediately.
02 / Investigations
  • FBC, UEC, LFT, calcium, coagulation and cancer-specific tests without delaying imaging.
  • Bladder scan for retention.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • Urgent MRI whole spine with contrast.
  • CT if MRI unavailable or for bony stability/surgical planning.
  • Do not use plain X-ray to exclude cord compression.
04 / Management
  • Treat as an oncological emergency. Keep spinal precautions when instability is possible, give corticosteroid promptly when malignant compression is suspected and obtain urgent whole-spine MRI.
  • Contact spinal surgery and radiation oncology immediately. Definitive treatment is urgent decompression and stabilisation, radiotherapy, or both based on stability, tumour sensitivity and prognosis.
  • Provide analgesia, bladder and bowel management, VTE prevention and early rehabilitation.
05 / Differentials
  • Metastatic epidural spinal cord compression.
  • Epidural abscess or haematoma, vertebral fracture, primary spinal tumour.
  • Disc herniation, cauda equina and inflammatory myelitis.
11.02

Superior vena cava obstruction

Time-critical first
Clinical prioritySit upright, give oxygen if hypoxaemic and urgently involve oncology, respiratory and interventional teams. Secure the airway early if laryngeal or cerebral oedema develops.
01 / Bedside examination
  • A-E, oxygenation and assess airway/cerebral compromise.
  • Facial/neck/upper limb swelling, venous distension, dyspnoea, cough, headache, visual or neurological symptoms.
  • Avoid upper-limb venous access when possible; urgent oncology and respiratory/interventional review.
02 / Investigations
  • FBC, UEC, LFT, coagulation and tumour-specific tests after imaging.
  • Obtain tissue diagnosis when feasible before definitive cancer therapy, unless immediately life-threatening.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • Urgent contrast CT chest defines level, cause and collateral flow.
  • Venography during endovascular stenting.
  • CXR may show mediastinal mass but is insufficient.
04 / Management
  • Sit upright, give oxygen if hypoxaemic and urgently involve oncology, respiratory and interventional teams. Secure the airway early if laryngeal or cerebral oedema develops.
  • Obtain tissue diagnosis before treatment when the patient is stable, but do not delay life-saving intervention in severe compromise.
  • Definitive treatment may include endovascular stenting, radiotherapy, systemic anticancer therapy and anticoagulation when thrombosis is present.
05 / Differentials
  • Malignancy: lung cancer, lymphoma, metastatic mediastinal disease.
  • Thrombosis from central venous devices or hypercoagulability.
  • Fibrosing mediastinitis and other rare benign compression.
11.03

Acute malignancy-related hypercalcaemia

Time-critical first
Clinical priorityStop contributing calcium or vitamin D and restore intravascular volume with monitored isotonic fluid, avoiding overload.
01 / Bedside examination
  • A-E; assess dehydration, confusion, weakness, constipation, arrhythmia and renal impairment.
  • Review cancer type, medications and volume status.
  • Severe symptoms or markedly elevated calcium require urgent oncology/endocrine input.
02 / Investigations
  • Corrected and/or ionised calcium, UEC/eGFR, phosphate, magnesium, albumin, ECG.
  • PTH to confirm non-PTH-mediated process; PTHrP, vitamin D metabolites or myeloma tests selectively.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • Imaging to identify cancer burden or bone disease after stabilisation.
  • Renal imaging if obstruction or stones; ECG monitoring for severe cases.
04 / Management
  • Stop contributing calcium or vitamin D and restore intravascular volume with monitored isotonic fluid, avoiding overload.
  • Give an antiresorptive agent such as IV bisphosphonate or denosumab according to renal function and oncology guidance; calcitonin may provide rapid short-term lowering in severe cases.
  • Treat arrhythmia, renal failure and the underlying malignancy, with dialysis for selected refractory life-threatening cases.
05 / Differentials
  • Humoral PTHrP-mediated hypercalcaemia, osteolytic metastases, myeloma.
  • Vitamin D-mediated lymphoma.
  • Non-malignant mimics: primary hyperparathyroidism, drugs, granulomatous disease.

Breaking bad news

Focused clinical approach
Clinical priorityPrepare a private setting, confirm who should be present and assess what the patient understands and wants to know.
01 / Bedside examination
  • Prepare: confirm facts, privacy, time, interpreter, key supports and who the patient wants present.
  • Assess what the patient understands and how much detail they want.
  • Use a warning shot, plain language, pauses and empathic acknowledgement.
02 / Investigations
  • No diagnostic tests. Ensure the diagnosis and uncertainty have been checked before the conversation.
  • Clarify capacity and advance care documentation where relevant.
03 / Imaging and procedures
  • No imaging component. Show relevant results only when they help understanding and the patient wants this.
  • Document attendees, information given, emotional response, decisions and follow-up.
04 / Management
  • Prepare a private setting, confirm who should be present and assess what the patient understands and wants to know.
  • Give a warning statement, communicate in small clear chunks, pause for emotion and respond empathically.
  • Agree on immediate next steps, document the discussion, provide written contacts and arrange timely follow-up and multidisciplinary support.
05 / Differentials
  • Common pitfalls: jargon, information overload, false reassurance, rushing to solutions, removing hope or discussing prognosis as certainty.
  • Use SPIKES or a similar framework, then provide a clear next step and safety net.
